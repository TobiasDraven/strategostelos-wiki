# Battle of Ia Drang (1965) — The First Air Assault

> *"The surrounded platoon of B Company stood off numerous PAVN efforts to overrun it. Carrying their dead, their wounded, and their equipment the men of the platoon established a small 25 meter wide perimeter on a slight rise of ground and redistributed their ammunition under heavy enemy fire."*
> — Lt. Col. Harold G. Moore, After Action Report, 14-16 November 1965

On 14 November 1965, the 1st Battalion, 7th Cavalry Regiment, 1st Cavalry Division (Airmobile) was air-assaulted into Landing Zone X-Ray in the Ia Drang Valley, Central Highlands, South Vietnam. It was the first major battle between regular forces of the United States Army and the People's Army of Vietnam (PAVN). The battle lasted four days and fundamentally changed the character of the Vietnam War.

This case study examines the battle through the first-hand accounts of Lt. Col. Harold G. Moore (the battalion commander), Captain Ramon Nadal (A Company commander), and the official after-action report filed on 9 December 1965.

---

## The Plan

### Intelligence Failure

Higher headquarters provided information that inaccurately described a small NVA force with minimal willingness to engage in sustained direct contact. Moore's after-action report noted that the intelligence picture was wrong. The prisoner captured on the first day — a deserter from the NVA — revealed that three battalions were on Chu Pong mountain, anxious to kill Americans but as yet unable to find them.

Moore's plan was to land the entire battalion on a single landing zone rather than dispersing companies across multiple zones. His after-action report explained:

> *"I made my decision to land at X-RAY with TANGO and YANKEE as alternates to be used only on my order. I then issued an oral operation order."*

The landing was preceded by a 20-minute artillery preparation from two 105mm howitzer batteries at LZ Falcon, nine kilometres east. Aerial rocket artillery followed for thirty seconds, then escort gunships swept the area.

### The Landing

At 1048 hours on 14 November, B Company touched down on LZ X-Ray unopposed. Moore's command group consisted of himself, the Battalion Sergeant Major, the S-2, two radio operators, and a Montagnard interpreter named Mr. Nik.

Moore's after-action report described the terrain:

> *"The terrain was flat and consisted of scrub trees up to 100 feet high; thick elephant grass varying in height from one foot to five feet; and ant hills throughout the area up to eight feet high with thick brush and elephant grass on and around them."*

---

## The Battle

### First Contact

At approximately 1245 hours, lead elements of B Company began to engage in a fire fight of moderate intensity. By 1330, the B Company commander reported being attacked heavily by at least two companies of enemy and that his right platoon was in danger of being surrounded.

Moore ordered A Company to move up. Captain Ramon Nadal later recalled the moment:

> *"I heard deafening fire going off in the area of the first platoon when I got a call from Colonel Moore telling us to find that platoon and bring 'em back. I got my soldiers together in the creek bed — I thought they needed a little pepping up. They'd seen their buddies killed. 'We've got a platoon out there, and we're going to go get 'em,' I said."*

Nadal's account continued:

> *"We'd moved forward about 50 meters. Suddenly, in a grassy area, all hell broke loose. Unknown to us, the enemy had been crawling forward in that grass. In one respect, our launching the assault may have saved the battalion."*

### The Isolated Platoon

One platoon from B Company was cut off from the battalion for two days. Moore's after-action report described their situation:

> *"The surrounded platoon of B Company stood off numerous PAVN efforts to overrun it. Carrying their dead, their wounded, and their equipment the men of the platoon established a small 25 meter wide perimeter on a slight rise of ground and redistributed their ammunition under heavy enemy fire."*

### The Cost

The 1st Battalion suffered 79 killed and 121 wounded. The 2nd Battalion, 7th Cavalry, which marched overland to relieve, was ambushed near LZ Albany on 17 November and suffered 155 killed. Together, these actions constituted the bloodiest single engagement of the Vietnam War to that point.

Nadal recalled:

> *"I came in with 95 guys. Eighteen were killed, and another 26 were wounded in two days."*

## Lessons for Landward Operations

### 1. Intelligence Is Often Wrong

Moore's after-action report noted that higher headquarters provided inaccurate intelligence. The assumption that the NVA would not engage in sustained direct contact proved fatally wrong. Commanders must plan for the possibility that the enemy will act contrary to expectations.

### 2. Mission Command Enables Adaptation

Moore could not directly command all facets of the battle. His subordinates exercised disciplined initiative within the limits of their orders. The isolated platoon's ability to establish a 25-meter perimeter and redistribute ammunition under fire was a direct result of mission command.

### 3. Firepower and Maneuver Must Be Integrated

The integration of tube artillery, aerial rocket artillery, gunships, and close air support was essential to the battalion's survival. Moore's after-action report emphasised the importance of "skillful positioning of his forces and judicious use of reserves."

### 4. The Air Assault Changes the Calculus

The helicopter enabled the battalion to be inserted quickly into a contested landing zone. But it also meant that the battalion was dependent on the helicopters for resupply and reinforcement. When the LZ came under heavy fire, subsequent lifts were delayed.

### 5. The Enemy Gets a Vote

Moore's initial concept — search and destroy a weak, fleeing enemy — was proven wrong within hours. The NVA chose to stand and fight. Moore adapted, developing and executing three separate command concepts over 36 hours: defending the LZ, organising the attack, and withdrawing the force.
