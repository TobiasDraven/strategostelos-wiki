# The Bangladesh Liberation War (1971) — Irregular Warfare and the Lightning Campaign

> *"The manoeuvre executed by crossing the mighty Meghna River posed a significant threat to Dacca, which made the Pakistani position militarily untenable."*
> — J.S. Cheema, *India's Politico-Military Strategy for the 1971 India–Pakistan War*

## Background

The Bangladesh Liberation War began on 25 March 1971 when the Pakistan Army launched Operation Searchlight, a brutal crackdown on East Pakistan's Bengali population following the Awami League's electoral victory. By May, an estimated 10 million refugees had flooded into India, creating an economic and humanitarian crisis that pushed India toward military intervention.[^1]

## The Strategic Debate

India's military planning was marked by a fundamental disagreement over objectives. The Army Headquarters' initial plan, formulated by Major General K.K. Singh (Director of Military Operations), focused on capturing the major ports of Chittagong and Khulna, assuming Dacca would "automatically fall."[^2]

Major General J.F.R. Jacob, Chief of Staff Eastern Command, disagreed. He argued that Dacca was the "geopolitical heart" of East Pakistan and its capture was imperative. When General Sam Manekshaw (Army Chief) asked Jacob if taking Khulna and Chittagong would cause Dacca to fall, Jacob replied: "I do not agree, and I reiterate that Dacca should be the key objective."[^3]

The final plan was a compromise — Dacca was not formally declared the primary objective, but enterprising commanders down the chain of command never lost sight of it.

## The Campaign

The war began on 3 December 1971 when Pakistan launched pre-emptive air strikes on Indian airfields. India responded with a multi-pronged offensive across four sectors:

**Western Sector**: 2 Corps captured Jessore on 7 December but failed to exploit momentum toward the deep objectives of Faridpur Crossing and Golundo Ghat Ferry — a strategic error that delayed the advance on Dacca.[^4]

**Eastern Sector**: Lieutenant General Sagat Singh's 4 Corps made rapid progress, bypassing Pakistani strongholds. By 9 December, Sylhet was surrounded and the Meghna bulge (Ashuganj, Dhaudkandi, Chandpur) was secured.

**The Meghna Crossing**: The decisive manoeuvre. On 9 December, 4 Guards was heli-lifted across the Meghna River using Mi-4 helicopters — 27 sorties by day, 30 by night. By 11 December, an entire brigade and artillery regiment had been airlifted (135 sorties, 1,628 troops landed).[^5]

**The Psychological Collapse**: On 14 December, the Indian Air Force bombed the governor's house in Dacca. A rumour spread that 5,000 paratroopers had landed at Tangail (it was a battalion group). The governor resigned. On 16 December, Major General Jacob flew to Dacca and secured the surrender of Lieutenant General A.A.K. Niazi — 93,000 troops, the largest capitulation since World War II.[^6]

## Lessons

1. **The centre of gravity**: Dacca was the geopolitical and geopolitical centre of gravity. Its capture rendered the entire Pakistani defence untenable, even though most of their forces were still intact.
2. **Mission command**: The "maverick commanders" — Jacob, Sagat Singh, Kler — succeeded because they adapted the plan to seize opportunities rather than adhering to a rigid attrition strategy.
3. **Helicopter mobility**: The Meghna crossing demonstrated the transformative potential of helicopter-borne forces in riverine terrain.
4. **Psychological warfare**: The rumour of a massive paratrooper drop (when only a battalion was involved) accelerated the Pakistani collapse.
5. **Mukti Bahini integration**: The Bangladeshi guerrilla force provided intelligence, harassed isolated posts, and acted as a force multiplier.
