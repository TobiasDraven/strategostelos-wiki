# Digital Financial Services — Cyber Resilience in Sub-Saharan Africa

> *"Over 40 per cent of adults in Sub-Saharan Africa now use mobile money. This is the highest adoption rate in the world."*
> — GSMA, *State of the Industry Report on Mobile Money* (2024)

Sub-Saharan Africa leads the world in mobile money adoption. Over 40 per cent of adults in the region use mobile money services. Mobile money has revolutionised financial inclusion, enabling millions of unbanked citizens to access financial services. But this digital transformation has also created new cybersecurity risks.

This page examines the nature of digital financial services (DFS) in Sub-Saharan Africa, the cyber threats they face, and the regulatory frameworks being developed to address them.

---

## The Mobile Money Revolution

### Financial Inclusion

Mobile money has been the primary driver of financial inclusion in Sub-Saharan Africa. The World Global Findex Database shows that account ownership in the region has more than doubled since 2011, driven largely by mobile money. In countries like Kenya, Uganda, Ghana, and Tanzania, mobile money accounts exceed traditional bank accounts.

Mobile money enables:
- **Remittances:** Domestic and international money transfers
- **Savings:** Secure storage of funds
- **Credit:** Micro-loans and emergency credit
- **Insurance:** Micro-insurance for health, crops, and livestock
- **Bill payments:** Utility payments and government services

### Economic Significance

Mobile money is a significant contributor to GDP. In Kenya, mobile money transactions exceed 50 percent of GDP. In Tanzania, mobile money is the largest component of the digital economy. The mobile money industry in Sub-Saharan Africa processes over USD 800 billion annually.

## The Cyber Threat Landscape

### Common Threats

The ITU Cyber Resilience Toolkit for DFS identifies the most common threats to digital financial services in Sub-Saharan Africa:

- **Credential attacks:** Phishing, social engineering, and credential theft
- **Systems and platform attacks:** Malware, ransomware, and denial of service
- **Code exploitation:** Exploitation of vulnerabilities in mobile applications and APIs
- **Data misuse:** Unauthorised access to customer data
- **SIM attacks:** SIM swap fraud and SIM jacking
- **DFS infrastructure attacks:** Attacks on mobile network infrastructure
- **DFS services attacks:** Fraudulent transactions and account takeovers

### The SIM Swap Epidemic

SIM swap fraud is particularly prevalent in Sub-Saharan Africa. Criminals impersonate customers at mobile network operator shops, obtain replacement SIM cards, and then drain mobile money accounts. The reliance on SIM-based authentication makes this attack vector especially dangerous.

### The Insider Threat

Insider threats are significant. Employees of mobile network operators and financial institutions have access to customer data and systems. Collusion between insiders and criminal networks facilitates fraud and data theft.

## Regulatory Responses

### National Frameworks

Several African countries have developed cybersecurity regulations for digital financial services:

- **Kenya:** The Central Bank of Kenya issued cybersecurity guidelines for payment service providers
- **Nigeria:** The Central Bank of Nigeria issued the Risk-Based Cybersecurity Framework for Deposit Money Banks
- **Ghana:** The Bank of Ghana issued the Cyber and Information Security Directive for financial institutions
- **Uganda:** The Bank of Uganda issued the Cybersecurity Regulations for payment service providers

### Regional Frameworks

Regional economic communities are developing harmonised approaches:

- **ECOWAS:** The ECOWAS Directive on Cybercrime and Cybersecurity
- **AU:** The Convention on Cyber Security and Personal Data Protection (Malabo Convention)
- **COMESA:** The Model Cybersecurity Framework for financial services

### The ITU Toolkit

The ITU Cyber Resilience Toolkit for DFS provides a guide for regulators to assess cybersecurity risks in digital finance infrastructure. It focuses on:
- Risk identification and assessment
- Policy and regulatory frameworks
- Incident response and recovery
- Capacity building and awareness

## Challenges

### Capacity Constraints

Most African regulators lack the technical expertise to effectively supervise cybersecurity in the financial sector. The rapid pace of technological change makes it difficult for regulators to keep up.

### Fragmentation

The regulatory landscape is fragmented across countries and regions. Different regulations, different enforcement capacities, and different levels of cybersecurity maturity create gaps that criminals exploit.

### The Innovation-Security Tension

Regulators must balance the need for innovation with the need for security. Overly restrictive regulations can stifle the growth of digital financial services. Under-regulation exposes consumers to cyber risks.

### Cross-Border Nature of Cybercrime

Cybercriminals operate across borders. A fraudster in one country can target customers in another. Cross-border enforcement is hampered by differences in legal frameworks, limited mutual legal assistance, and slow judicial processes.

---

## Lessons for the Cyber Domain in Africa

### 1. DFS Is Critical Infrastructure

Digital financial services are now critical infrastructure. Their disruption can have systemic economic effects. They must be protected accordingly.

### 2. SIM-Based Authentication Is Vulnerable

The reliance on SIM-based authentication creates a single point of failure. Multi-factor authentication and biometric verification are needed to reduce SIM swap fraud.

### 3. Regional Harmonisation Is Essential

Fragmented regulations create gaps. Regional harmonisation of cybersecurity frameworks can reduce these gaps and improve cross-border enforcement.

### 4. Capacity Building Is Urgent

Regulators, financial institutions, and law enforcement all need cybersecurity capacity. This requires sustained investment in training, tools, and technical assistance.

### 5. Public Awareness Is Critical

Consumers are often the weakest link in the cybersecurity chain. Public awareness campaigns can reduce the effectiveness of phishing, social engineering, and other consumer-targeted attacks.
