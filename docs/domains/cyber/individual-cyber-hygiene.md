# Individual Cyber Hygiene — The Soldier's Digital Defence

> *"Cyber hygiene should be a daily routine, just like cleaning weapons or checking gear."*
> — Kateryna Chernohorenko, Deputy Minister of Defence for Digital Development, Ukraine

---

## The Soldier as First Line of Defence

Every soldier is a cyber sentinel. Your daily digital practices protect not only your personal data but also your unit's operational security. The enemy does not need to hack a satellite. They need one soldier to click a bad link.

This page covers the cyber hygiene every soldier must practise. It focuses on the individual behaviours that prevent the most common attacks.

---

## The Threat to Individuals

### Phishing

Phishing is the most common attack against military personnel. The attacker sends a message that looks real. It asks you to click a link, open an attachment, or enter your credentials.

Common phishing attempts targeting soldiers:
- A message that appears to be from a commander, asking you to "update your data" immediately.
- A message from a "bank" or "government agency" asking you to verify your account.
- A message with an attachment labelled "urgent" or "for your eyes only."

**Protect yourself:**
- Do not click links in unexpected messages. Type the address manually.
- Verify the sender through another channel if you are suspicious.
- Look for digital signatures on official emails.
- Never give out personal or financial information by email.

### Spear Phishing and Whaling

Spear phishing targets you specifically. The attacker uses your name, rank, and details from social media to craft a believable message. Whaling targets senior officers with highly personalised attacks.

**Protect yourself:**
- Be wary of emails that use your name but ask for sensitive information.
- Report suspicious messages to your security point of contact.
- Remember: legitimate organisations do not ask for passwords by email.

### Smishing and Vishing

Smishing uses text messages. Vishing uses voice calls. Both try to trick you into giving up information or installing malicious software.

**Protect yourself:**
- Do not reply to suspicious text messages. Delete them.
- Let unknown calls go to voicemail. Legitimate callers will leave a message.
- Never give out one-time verification codes to anyone who calls you.

## Passwords and Authentication

### Strong Passwords

Weak passwords are easy to crack. Use these rules:
- Minimum 15 characters.
- Mix uppercase, lowercase, numbers, and special characters.
- Do not reuse passwords across different services.
- Do not use personal information (birth dates, family names, unit identifiers).
- Use a password manager to generate and store strong passwords.

### Two-Factor Authentication

Enable two-factor authentication on all critical accounts. This adds a second verification step. Even if an attacker steals your password, they cannot access your account without the second factor.

## Mobile Device Security

### Physical Security

- Keep your device locked when not in use, even for a few minutes.
- Do not leave devices unattended in public places.
- Report stolen devices immediately to law enforcement and your service provider.
- Reset devices to factory settings before disposing of them.

### Software and Apps

- Update your operating system and apps regularly. Updates patch known vulnerabilities.
- Only install apps from official sources (App Store, Google Play, verified developer websites).
- Check app permissions before installing. Do not give apps access to contacts, location, or camera unless necessary.
- Disable Bluetooth and Wi-Fi when not in use.

### Public Wi-Fi

Public Wi-Fi networks are not secure. Avoid them for sensitive activities. If you must use them:
- Use a Virtual Private Network (VPN).
- Only visit sites with encrypted connections (https://).
- Do not conduct online banking or shopping on public networks.

## Social Media and OPSEC

### The Digital Footprint

Everything you post online creates a permanent record. Adversaries use open-source intelligence (OSINT) to harvest your photos, biographies, and location data. They use this information to impersonate you, target your family, or plan attacks.

**Protect yourself and your unit:**
- Do not post personally identifiable information (PII): date of birth, home address, family relations.
- Do not post photos of yourself in uniform with identifiable landmarks.
- Do not use check-in features or post your specific location.
- Do not accept friend requests from people you do not know.
- Validate identities before connecting.
- Be cautious about images you post. Background details can reveal more than you intend.

### Social Engineering on Social Media

Scammers create fake profiles using stolen photos of service members. They target vulnerable people with romance scams or advance-fee schemes. Once you stop sending money, the scammer disappears.

**Protect yourself:**
- Verify the identity of anyone you meet online.
- Do not send money to people you have not met in person.
- Report suspicious profiles to the platform and your security point of contact.

## Reporting Incidents

If you become a victim of a cyberattack, report it immediately. Prompt reporting can prevent the threat from spreading to others.

**What to do:**
- Change your passwords immediately.
- Retain all evidence: screenshots, links, suspicious files.
- Report to your security point of contact or help desk.
- If personal data is compromised unrelated to official duties, report to the relevant civilian authorities.
