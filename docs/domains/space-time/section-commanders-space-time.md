# Section Commander's Space-Time — Reading Ground, Time, and Intent

> *"Get there firstest with the mostest."*
> — Confederate General Nathan Bedford Forrest

The section commander operates at the lowest level of military command. Decisions are immediate. The consequences are direct. There is no time for detailed analysis. The section commander must read the ground, understand time pressure, and act on intent.

This page discusses the space-time domain at the tactical level most relevant to junior leaders: the lance corporal and corporal commanding a fire team or section.

---

## Reading the Ground

Terrain is the physical framework within which every engagement occurs. The section commander must read terrain quickly and accurately.

### Key Terrain Features

Terrain is analysed using the military mnemonic **OCOKA**:

- **Observation:** Where can you see the enemy? Where can the enemy see you? Good observation enables effective fire and early warning. Poor observation creates surprise.

- **Cover and concealment:** Cover protects from fire. Concealment hides from observation. The section commander must identify both and use them to protect the section.

- **Obstacles:** Natural or artificial features that impede movement. Rivers, walls, minefields, wire. Obstacles can protect flanks or block routes of advance.

- **Key terrain:** Any feature whose seizure, retention, or control provides a marked advantage to either side. A hilltop. A bridge. A road junction. Key terrain is the spatial focus of the battle.

- **Avenues of attack:** Routes along which a force can move to reach its objective. The section commander must identify covered and concealed avenues that allow the section to close with the enemy.

### Ground and Movement

The section commander must calculate not merely the distance to an objective, but the time required to traverse that distance in the specific terrain. A kilometre of open ground takes minutes. A kilometre of dense vegetation takes longer. A kilometre under fire takes longest of all.

Ground also determines the formation the section adopts. Open terrain may allow a wide skirmish line. Jungle requires a single file. Urban terrain requires bounding overwatch between buildings.

## Understanding Time

Time at the section level is measured in seconds and minutes. The section commander must understand three temporal factors:

### 1. Time Available

How long before the section must be at the objective? How long before the enemy will arrive? How long before the supporting artillery lifts? The section commander works backwards from these deadlines to determine what is possible.

### 2. Time to Move

The section commander must estimate movement time based on terrain, load, and conditions. A fit section moves at approximately four kilometres per hour on roads. This drops to two kilometres per hour cross-country and one kilometre per hour in dense vegetation. Under fire, movement is slower still.

### 3. Time to React

The most critical temporal factor. When contact occurs, the section commander must recognise the threat and issue orders within seconds. The faster the section reacts, the more likely it is to survive and win.

Boyd's OODA loop applies at the section level. The section that observes the enemy first, orients correctly, decides quickly, and acts decisively gains the initiative. The section that hesitates loses.

---

## Acting on Intent

The section commander receives orders from the platoon commander. These orders include the commander's intent — the purpose of the operation and the desired end state. The section commander must understand intent well enough to act when the plan changes.

Intent at the section level is simple. "Seize that hilltop." "Defend this position." "Provide covering fire for the platoon advance." The section commander must be able to achieve the intent even when communication fails or the situation changes.

This requires **mission command** at the lowest level. The platoon commander states the intent. The section commander decides how to achieve it. If the planned route is blocked, the section commander selects an alternative. If the enemy appears from an unexpected direction, the section commander reacts without waiting for orders.

---

## The Section Commander's Decision Cycle

The section commander's decision cycle is compressed. There is no time for detailed analysis. The cycle is:

1. **Observe:** Detect the enemy. Identify terrain features. Note changes in the situation.

2. **Orient:** Match what you see with what you know. Is this the expected enemy? Is this terrain as expected? Does this change the plan?

3. **Decide:** Choose a course of action. The section commander has limited options: advance, defend, delay, or withdraw. The decision must be quick.

4. **Act:** Issue fire orders. Direct movement. Control the section. The action must be immediate.

This cycle repeats continuously throughout the engagement. The section commander who cycles faster than the enemy section gains the advantage.

---

## Space-Time and the Junior Leader

The lance corporal does not plan campaigns. But the lance corporal must understand space and time at the most fundamental level. Where am I? Where is the enemy? Where is my objective? How long will it take to get there? How long do I have? What happens if I am late?

These questions are the foundation of tactical decision-making. The section commander who can answer them quickly and accurately is the section commander who wins.
