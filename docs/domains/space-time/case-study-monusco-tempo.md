# Tempo in African Peace Ops: MONUSCO

> *"Time is the most constrained resource in war. Unlike ammunition or fuel, time cannot be stockpiled. Once lost, it cannot be recovered."*
> — Space-Time Domain, *Strategos Telos*

## The Space-Time Problem in MONUSCO

The United Nations Organization Stabilization Mission in the Democratic Republic of the Congo (MONUSCO) operated in one of Africa's most spatially vast and temporally complex conflict environments. Eastern DRC covers terrain the size of Western Europe. Armed groups—M23, ADF, FDLR, CODECO—moved across porous borders with impunity. Civilians were displaced in days. The mission's mandate required it to protect civilians, support stabilisation, and assist security sector reform across this expanse.

The space-time problem was structural: MONUSCO could not be everywhere at once. Its tempo was dictated by logistics, politics, and the initiative of the enemy.

## The Mandate and Its Temporal Constraints

MONUSCO's mandate, renewed annually by the UN Security Council, imposed a rhythm that conflicted with operational tempo. Mandrup notes that the mission was simultaneously expected to stabilise vast territory, support political processes, and respond to emergency civilian protection tasks—without the forces or political authority to do all three at once.

The Force Intervention Brigade (FIB), established in 2013, represented a deliberate tempo shift. Mandated to conduct offensive operations, the FIB defeated M23 in 2013 within days of engagement. This demonstrated that tempo advantage—concentrated combat power applied rapidly—could achieve decisive results when political authority permitted.

However, the FIB's tempo could not be sustained. Subsequent M23 resurgences in 2022 exposed the gap between kinetic tempo and political patience. Vrey's framework for tempo in the African battlespace identifies this as the central tension: African security operations are conducted under intense temporal pressure from civilians and political principals, while the operational environment resists speed through terrain, logistics, and institutional friction.

## Friction and the Loss of Tempo

Clausewitz's concept of friction manifested in MONUSCO through multiple layers:

1. **Logistical friction.** Supply lines across Central Africa were long, fragile, and vulnerable to ambush. The MONUSCO Military Logistics Base at Entebbe depended on air and river corridors that could be disrupted.
2. **Political friction.** The UN Security Council's incremental approach to mandate adaptation meant that force posture often lagged behind events on the ground. When M23 advanced in 2022, the mission's rules of engagement and force structure required weeks to adjust.
3. **Institutional friction.** Coordination between MONUSCO, the East African Community Regional Force (EACRF), and the Southern African Development Community Mission in the DRC (SAMIDRC) introduced additional temporal delays. Mandrup documents how competition between regional actors for influence in the DRC slowed operational synchronisation.
4. **Emission and information friction.** The electromagnetic environment in eastern DRC was contested. Insurgent radio networks, cell-phone-based command systems, and information operations created a dense electromagnetic battlespace that degraded both MONUSCO and adversary tempo.

## The EACRF and SAMIDRC Transitions

When MONUSCO began its withdrawal from South Kivu in December 2023, the transition to regional forces introduced new tempo challenges. EACRF's mandate was short and its exit rapid. SAMIDRC—led by South Africa—assumed a more offensive posture, but Mandrup notes that the South African National Defence Force (SANDF) faced significant resource constraints that affected deployment speed and operational sustainability.

The tempo of regional force deployment was slower than the tempo of insurgent adaptation. M23 exploited the security vacuum during transition periods. This pattern—withdrawal, gap, re-entry—repeated across eastern DRC from 2013 through 2024, demonstrating that tempo transitions are themselves a critical vulnerability in peace operations.

## Tempo and Civilian Protection

The central MONUSCO task—protecting civilians—was the most temporally sensitive. Civilians displaced by violence required immediate response. MONUSCO's forward operating bases, quick reaction forces, and community alert networks were designed to compress the response-to-threat timeline. However, Vrey's analysis of African peace operations shows that civilian protection tempo is frequently frustrated by:

- **Distance.** MONUSCO bases were often tens or hundreds of kilometres from the populations at risk.
- **Terrain.** Poor road networks in North Kivu and Ituri slowed ground movement.
- **Rules of engagement.** Escalation procedures required headquarters approval that could not keep pace with unfolding atrocities.
- **Information lag.** Reports of attacks reached MONUSCO through community networks that depended on cellular coverage and civilian courage.

The result was a persistent tempo gap between threat and response—a gap that insurgent groups learned to exploit.

## Lessons from MONUSCO for African Peace Ops

1. **Tempo is a mandate function.** Peace operations require mandates that allow rapid response without incremental political approval for each engagement. The FIB demonstrated what is possible when authority matches operational tempo.
2. **Regional transitions are tempo events.** Withdrawal or handover periods are predictable windows of vulnerability. Planning for these transitions must include tempo-sustaining measures—overlapping deployment, reserve forces, and emergency bridging capacities.
3. **EMCON discipline affects protection tempo.** In an environment where insurgents use commercial communications, the electromagnetic domain directly shapes the speed of threat detection and response. EW and signals intelligence must be embedded in civilian protection task forces.
4. **African solutions must account for African friction.** Vrey argues that African peace operations face friction patterns distinct from UN or NATO contexts: logistics across vast territory, political pressures from multiple regional actors, and the persistence of asymmetric threats that exploit tempo gaps. Strategies imported from other contexts fail if they do not address these specific constraints.
