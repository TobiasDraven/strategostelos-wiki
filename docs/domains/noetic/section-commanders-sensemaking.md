# Section Commander's Sensemaking — Reading Ground, Enemy, and Intent

> *"Sensemaking is the process by which people interpret ambiguous situations and decide how to act."*
> — Weick, *Sensemaking in Organizations*, 1995

---

## The Cognitive Work of the Junior Leader

A section commander (lance corporal or corporal) operates at the lowest tactical level. Your cognitive work is immediate: read the ground, read the enemy, understand your commander's intent, and act. You do not have time for extended analysis. You must recognise patterns quickly and decide under stress.

This page covers the noetic demands of junior leadership. It focuses on practical sensemaking — what to look for, what to ignore, and how to communicate clearly when contact starts.

---

## Reading Terrain

### OCOKA

Use the five factors of OCOKA to analyse terrain quickly:

1. **Observation and fields of fire.** Where can you see the enemy? Where can the enemy see you? Identify positions that give your section good observation without exposing you to enemy fire.

2. **Cover and concealment.** Cover protects you from fire. Concealment hides you from observation. A position can have concealment without cover (behind a bush in the open) or cover without concealment (a low wall the enemy can see over). Find positions with both.

3. **Obstacles.** Natural or man-made features that stop, slow, or channel movement. Obstacles can befriend you if they block enemy approaches. They can hurt you if they trap your section in a kill zone.

4. **Key terrain.** Any feature that gives a marked advantage to whoever controls it. At section level, key terrain may be a small rise, a crossing point, or a building that dominates the area.

5. **Avenues of approach.** Routes along which an enemy force can move. Identify the most likely and most dangerous avenues. Position your section to cover them.

### Terrain Association

Use terrain association to navigate and to maintain situational awareness. Match the contour intervals on your map to the features on the ground. Identify ridgelines, valleys, and man-made structures along your route. Update your position mentally as you move. Do not rely only on a GPS.

## Reading the Enemy

### Pattern Recognition

Enemy forces follow patterns. They use standard formations, timing, and tactics. Learn the patterns for the threat you face. Look for:

- **Signs of presence.** Tracks, disturbed vegetation, fresh digging, smoke, or noise.
- ** Likely positions.** The enemy will use the same logic you do. They want cover, concealment, good observation, and fields of fire. If you identify the best positions on the ground, the enemy probably has too.
- **Indicators of intent.** Movement in a particular direction, digging in, or reconnaissance activity can reveal what the enemy plans to do.

### Recognition-Primed Decision Making

Klein's research shows that experienced leaders do not compare many options. They recognise the situation as similar to one they have seen before. They mentally simulate a course of action. If it works, they act. If not, they adjust.

At section level, this means:

- **Identify the type of situation.** Is this an ambush? A hasty defence? A meeting engagement?
- **Mentally rehearse your response.** Before you move, imagine what will happen. Check for obvious flaws.
- **Act decisively.** Once you decide, move. Delay kills.

## Understanding Intent

### Commander's Intent

Your platoon commander's intent tells you what the platoon must do and why. Your job is to understand it well enough to act when the plan changes. In contact, you may not receive orders. You must know what the commander wants to achieve. Then you can make the right decision.

At section level, understand:

- **The section's task.** What must your section do?
- **The purpose.** Why must you do it?
- **The commander's desired end state.** What does success look like?

### Sense-Giving

You are not only a sensemaker. You are also a sense-giver. You must communicate your understanding to your section. Tell them what you see, what you think it means, and what you want them to do. Clear, calm communication under fire is a cognitive skill. It requires practice.

## Communicating Under Stress

### Reporting

When you report to your platoon commander, follow a simple format:

1. **What you see.** Describe the enemy or terrain feature.
2. **Where it is.** Give a grid reference or a clear description.
3. **What you think it means.** Give your assessment.
4. **What you plan to do.** State your intended action.

Keep reports short. Use correct terminology. Do not guess. If you do not know something, say so.

### Managing Cognitive Load

Under stress, your working memory shrinks. You can hold fewer ideas at once. To manage this:

- **Use checklists.** Memorise the key checks for your section (ammunition, casualties, position, enemy).
- **Chunk information.** Group related facts together. Instead of reporting three separate enemy positions, report one enemy section in a specific area.
- **Breathe.** Controlled breathing reduces stress and restores cognitive function.

---

## Training Your Sensemaking

Sensemaking is a trainable skill. To improve:

- **Practice terrain analysis.** On every patrol or exercise, analyse the ground using OCOKA. Do it until it becomes automatic.
- **Study enemy tactics.** Learn how the enemy fights. The more patterns you know, the faster you will recognise them.
- **Do decision exercises.** Use tactical decision games (TDGs) to practise recognising situations and choosing responses.
- **Get feedback.** After every exercise, ask your platoon commander or section members what you missed. Learn from it.
