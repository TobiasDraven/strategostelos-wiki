# Sensemaking in the Rwandan Defence Force

> *"Sensemaking is the central function of military command."*
> — Brehmer, *Good Sensemaking is More Important than Information for the Quality of Plans*

## The Sensemaking Imperative

The Rwanda Defence Force (RDF) emerged from circumstances that made sensemaking a matter of survival. The 1994 Genocide against the Tutsi was, in part, a catastrophic failure of collective sensemaking by the international community and by Rwandan state institutions. The RPF/RPA advance that ended the genocide was driven by a small group of commanders who had to make sense of a collapsing state, a hostile population, and a regional war while executing operational manoeuvre.

This origin experience shaped the RDF's approach to sensemaking as an institutional priority—not an abstract cognitive process, but a survival skill encoded into doctrine, training, and leadership development.

## The Strategic Culture of Sensemaking

The RDF's sensemaking framework derives from a coherent strategic culture forged under President Paul Kagame. The Rwandan MoD's official history describes a force whose "ideology as a force that is true to the ideals developed under the Chairman of the High Command during the liberation war" provides the interpretive lens through which operational situations are assessed.

This strategic culture operates as a sensemaking apparatus in three ways:

1. **Narrative coherence.** The RDF's self-understanding—as a people's military, a development force, and a regional security provider—provides a stable frame for interpreting ambiguous situations. Every operation is assessed against this narrative, reducing the cognitive load of sensemaking under stress.

2. **Anticipatory cognition.** Kagame's strategic culture, as analysed by Clark and others, emphasises pre-emptive action. The RDF does not wait for situations to clarify; it acts to shape them. This is sensemaking as intervention, not mere interpretation.

3. **Moral clarity as cognitive anchor.** The RDF's explicit framing of its purpose—preventing genocide, protecting civilians, supporting development—provides a normative anchor for decision-making under conditions of extreme ambiguity. Commanders are not required to interpret intent from vague higher-headquarters guidance; they operate within a stated strategic purpose.

## Institutionalised Sensemaking: Education and Doctrine

The RDF has invested heavily in institutionalising sensemaking through its command and staff education system. The RDF Senior Command and Staff College and the Rwanda National Defence College produce graduates who "conceptualize and analyse security problems in a military perspective, so as to make timely and logical decisions in the contemporary volatile, uncertain and complex operating environment."

The RDF Establishment—the force's internal structural and doctrinal document—incorporates operational experience from "decades of conducting past military operations in wide-ranging environments" and is described as "forward-looking but also maintains core values and ethos." This dual character—rooted in specific historical experience while designed for future contingency—is a sensemaking mechanism: it allows the RDF to interpret novel situations through a tested doctrinal lens while retaining the flexibility to adapt.

## Operational Sensemaking: Lessons from UNAMIR to Cabo Delgado

The RDF's approach to sensemaking in operations can be traced through its peacekeeping evolution.

### UNAMIR and the Sensemaking Failure

The 1994 UNAMIR mission in Rwanda represents, from the RDF perspective, a paradigmatic sensemaking failure. The mission's mandate was anchored to the Arusha Accords—a political framework that became irrelevant once the genocide began. RDF officers who observed or participated in UNAMIR (as RPA combatants or later as RDF peacekeepers) learned that rigid mandates, disconnected from ground realities, produce institutional sensemaking failure.

The lesson, internalised in RDF doctrine, is that strategy must precede mandate. As one analysis notes, the RDF "witnessed peacekeepers' failures that were beyond just failed political will but instead its strategy. The UNAMIR forces deployed in Rwanda prior to the Genocide against the Tutsi held the strategic goal of upholding and carrying out the public policy of the 1993 Arusha Accords. It could not adapt to Rwanda's changing environment."

### Cabo Delgado: Sensemaking in Counterinsurgency

The RDF deployment to Cabo Delgado Province, Mozambique (from 2021), demonstrated institutionalised sensemaking at scale. The RDF's approach, as documented by researchers including Shield and Clark, integrated civilian protection with counterinsurgency in a way that reflected deliberate strategic choice rather than ad hoc adaptation.

Key sensemaking features of the Cabo Delgado deployment:
- **Frame stability.** The RDF operated from a stable strategic frame—protecting civilians from insurgent violence—that did not shift with tactical circumstances.
- **Distributed sensemaking.** RDF officers in Mozambique exercised initiative within the strategic frame. The RDF's command education system had prepared them for volatile, complex environments where higher-headquarters direction would be delayed or absent.
- **Cultural sensemaking.** The RDF's experience in Rwanda gave it a particular interpretive lens for situations of societal collapse and mass atrocities. This lens shaped how RDF forces assessed the Cabo Delgado insurgency and what actions they deemed appropriate.

## Sensemaking and Civil-Military Relations

The RDF's sensemaking apparatus operates within a specific civil-military framework. The Rwandan Constitution assigns the RDF a dual mission: defending territorial integrity and supporting national development. This dual mission creates a broader sensemaking frame than most African militaries possess. The RDF does not merely interpret tactical situations; it interprets its role within national transformation.

This broad frame has risks. Critics note that the RDF's strategic culture is a top-down process, closely associated with Kagame's beliefs, and that alternative sensemaking frames—particularly those that challenge the regime's narrative—are institutionally excluded. The RDF's sensemaking strength (coherence, speed, moral clarity) is also its potential vulnerability (groupthink, limited dissent, constrained frame enumeration).

## Comparative Significance

The RDF's approach to sensemaking is distinctive in the African context. Most African militaries operate with fragmented doctrine, limited professional military education, and civil-military relations that constrain institutional cognitive development. The RDF has deliberately addressed all three.

The result is a military that can make sense of complex, ambiguous situations faster and with greater coherence than most regional peers. This sensemaking advantage is evident in operational performance—from the speed of the 1994 campaign to the effectiveness of RDF deployments in CAR, Mozambique, and Benin.

## Implications

1. **Sensemaking is trainable.** The RDF demonstrates that sensemaking can be institutionalised through deliberate education, doctrine, and strategic culture development.
2. **Narrative coherence enables cognitive speed.** A stable strategic frame reduces the time required for collective sensemaking under pressure.
3. **Historical experience shapes interpretive frames.** The RDF's sensemaking apparatus is rooted in specific historical trauma. This is not universally replicable, but it illustrates how militaries can convert historical experience into cognitive advantage.
4. **Frame diversity matters.** The RDF's limitation—limited institutional space for competing sensemaking frames—is a risk for operations where the strategic environment has changed in ways the dominant frame cannot easily accommodate.
