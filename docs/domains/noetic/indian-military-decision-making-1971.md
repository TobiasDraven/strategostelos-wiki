# Indian Military Decision-Making — The 1971 War

> *"Strategy, per se, was not studied in the Indian Army. No military institution in India taught the subject, nor was there any strategic planning at army HQ. However, tactics up to brigade and, to some extent at the divisional level, were taught. Tactics may win battles, but it is strategy that wins wars."*
> — Lieutenant General J.F.R. Jacob, *An Odyssey in War and Peace*, 2011

## The Decision-Making Architecture

India's 1971 war was managed through an informal but highly effective decision-making structure. Prime Minister Indira Gandhi relied on a small core group of trusted bureaucrats — Principal Secretary P.N. Haksar, Chairman Policy Planning Committee D.P. Dhar, Defence Secretary K.B. Lall, Foreign Secretary T.N. Kaul, and External Intelligence Chief R.N. Kao — rather than the formal Parliamentary Affairs Committee.[^1]

The three Service Chiefs — General "Sam" Manekshaw (Army), Air Chief Marshal P.C. Lal (Air Force), and Admiral S.M. Nanda (Navy) — provided military advice. The key political trio of Gandhi, Defence Minister Jagjivan Ram, and Foreign Minister Swaran Singh weighed complex courses of action and adopted a strategy that leveraged the East Bengal crisis to militarily split Pakistan.[^1]

## The Strategic Debate

The most consequential decision-making failure was the disagreement over objectives. Major General J.F.R. Jacob, Chief of Staff Eastern Command, argued that Dacca was the "geopolitical and geostrategic heart" of East Pakistan and must be the primary objective. Major General K.K. Singh (Director of Military Operations) and General Manekshaw disagreed — they believed capturing the ports of Khulna and Chittagong would cause Dacca to "automatically fall."[^2]

Jacob's account of the August 1971 meeting at Fort William:

> *"Manekshaw smiled at me, using his favourite term of endearment, 'Jake sweetie, don't you see that if we take Khulna and Chittagong, Dacca will automatically fall. There is, therefore, no need to take Dacca.' Further heated exchanges took place with the DMO. Eventually, Manekshaw turned to Aurora for support, 'Jagjit, don't you agree that if we take Khulna and Chittagong, Dacca will automatically fall.' Aurora replied, 'Yes Sir, I entirely agree.'"*[^2]

The operational orders issued in August 1971 did not mention Dacca as an objective. Jacob was forced to work around Army Headquarters — moving three brigades from the Chinese border to the Dacca thrust without Manekshaw's knowledge, and planning the Tangail para drop in October without formal sanction.[^2]

## The Decision to Delay

In April 1971, the government wanted an immediate military response to the refugee crisis. General Manekshaw advised against it — citing the likely Chinese threat, the impending monsoons, and the time needed to build logistics. He told the government: "I will not be ready before 15th November."[^3]

This decision was controversial but correct. It gave India time to:
- Train and equip forces for riverine warfare
- Build infrastructure and logistics in Tripura and Meghalaya (30,000 tonnes moved to Tripura alone)
- Position forces for a winter campaign when Chinese passes would be closed
- Allow the Mukti Bahini to organize and train

## Mission Command in Practice

The war demonstrated both the power and the limits of mission command. Lieutenant General Sagat Singh (4 Corps) and Brigadier H.S. Kler (95 Mountain Brigade) seized opportunities that were not in the original plan. Sagat Singh's heliborne crossing of the Meghna — 350 sorties lifting three brigades — was not pre-planned but exploited the Pakistan Army's rapid collapse.[^4]

However, other commanders failed to exploit success. 2 Corps captured Jessore on 7 December but failed to push toward the deep objectives of Faridpur Crossing and Golundo Ghat Ferry — a strategic error that delayed the advance on Dacca.[^5]

## Lessons

1. **The centre of gravity**: Jacob was right — Dacca was the geopolitical and geostrategic centre of gravity. Its capture rendered the entire Pakistani defence untenable, even though most forces were still intact.
2. **Civil-military relations**: The informal decision-making structure worked because Gandhi gave her military chiefs a free hand. The credit for the victory belongs "most of all to Indira Gandhi, a master strategist and resolute leader, who reposed utmost faith in the armed forces leadership."[^1]
3. **Strategic education matters**: Jacob's observation that "strategy was not studied in the Indian Army" highlights a critical gap. The 1971 war was won despite, not because of, the Army's institutional strategic education.
4. **Intelligence and assumption**: Manekshaw's assumption that China would attack (held until 8 December) led to 6 Mountain Division being withheld from the eastern front. Intelligence indicated Chinese non-intervention, but the assumption prevailed.
5. **The psychological dimension**: The rumour of 5,000 paratroopers at Tangail (when only a battalion was involved) and the bombing of the governor's house accelerated the Pakistani collapse. Decision-makers must account for perception as well as reality.
