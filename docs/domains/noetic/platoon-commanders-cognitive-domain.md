# The Platoon Commander's Cognitive Domain — Translating Intent into Action

> *"The platoon leader must have a relatively complete knowledge of the terrain, and he must know as much as possible about the enemy."*
> — FM 17-98, *Scout Platoon*, U.S. Department of the Army

---

## The Cognitive Leap to Platoon Command

A lieutenant commands a platoon of 30 soldiers. The cognitive demand jumps sharply from section command. You now coordinate multiple sections. You maintain situational awareness across a larger area. You translate company commander's intent into platoon orders. You make decisions in contact when information is contradictory and time is short.

This page covers the noetic work of platoon command. It focuses on the cognitive demands that distinguish a lieutenant from a corporal.

---

## Situational Awareness at Platoon Level

### Three Levels of Awareness

Endsley's model defines three levels of situation awareness:

1. **Level 1 — Perception.** You see the relevant elements in the environment. At platoon level, this means knowing where your sections are, where the enemy is, and what the terrain looks like.

2. **Level 2 — Comprehension.** You understand what those elements mean. You recognise that the enemy is forming up for an attack, not just moving. You understand that a terrain feature blocks your fields of fire.

3. **Level 3 — Projection.** You predict what will happen next. You anticipate where the enemy will move. You foresee how the terrain will affect the battle in the next 30 minutes.

Research with infantry platoon leaders shows that experienced officers attend more to enemy locations and strengths. Less experienced officers attend more to friendly strengths. Train yourself to focus on the enemy and the terrain.

### Maintaining Awareness Across the Platoon

Your platoon operates dispersed. Each section sees a different piece of the battlefield. Your job is to build a coherent picture from these fragments. To do this:

- **Establish a reporting rhythm.** Tell sections when to report. Enforce it.
- **Use a map.** Plot friendly positions one level higher. Plot enemy spot reports as they come in.
- **Ask the right questions.** When a section reports, ask: What do you see? What do you hear? What do you think it means?

## Cognitive Load and Decision Making

### The Burden of Command

Research shows that platoon leaders face high cognitive workload. You monitor the tactical display. You listen to the radio. You observe the battlefield. You issue orders. You coordinate with adjacent platoons. All at once.

Studies on message presentation rate show that fast rates increase cognitive load and degrade situation awareness. Visual messages (text) create higher load than auditory ones (radio voice). When information comes too fast, you lose awareness and make worse decisions.

### Managing Your Load

To manage cognitive load:

- **Prioritise.** Not every piece of information needs your immediate attention. Filter.
- **Delegate.** Trust your platoon sergeant to handle administration and logistics. Focus on the fight.
- **Use your map.** Offload memory to the map. Plot positions instead of holding them in your head.
- **Pause when you can.** Even a 10-second pause helps you reassess.

## Translating Intent into Orders

### Understanding the Company Commander's Intent

The company commander's intent tells you what the company must achieve and why. Your job is to translate that intent into a platoon concept. You must understand:

- **The company end state.** What does success look like for the company?
- **The platoon task.** What must your platoon do to support the company?
- **The key tasks.** What must be achieved regardless of course of action?

### Giving Clear Orders

Your platoon order must be clear enough for section commanders to act when they cannot reach you. Use the standard format (SMEAC: Situation, Mission, Execution, Administration, Command). Keep it simple. Tell section commanders:

- **What the platoon will do.**
- **What their section will do.**
- **Why it matters.**
- **What to do if the plan changes.**

## The Platoon Sergeant Relationship

### Distributed Cognition

You and your platoon sergeant form a two-person command team. This is a distributed cognitive system. You focus on the enemy and the battle. He focuses on the platoon's readiness, ammunition, casualties, and logistics. Together, you maintain a more complete picture than either could alone.

To make this work:

- **Trust him.** Give him authority over administrative and logistical matters.
- **Communicate constantly.** Share what you see and what you think. Ask for his assessment.
- **Back him up.** When he makes a decision within his authority, support it.

## Making Decisions in Contact

### The OODA Loop Under Fire

In contact, your OODA loop compresses. You observe the enemy. You orient using your understanding of the terrain and the enemy. You decide on a course of action. You act. Then you start again.

Under stress, the loop can break. You may fixate on one piece of information. You may fail to update your assessment. To prevent this:

- **Keep asking questions.** What has changed? What am I missing?
- **Use your platoon sergeant.** He sees things you do not.
- **Accept risk.** You will never have perfect information. Decide with what you have.

### Recognition-Primed Decisions

Klein's research shows that experienced leaders recognise situations and respond almost automatically. They do not compare options. They recognise the pattern. They mentally simulate a response. If it works, they act.

To build this skill:

- **Study past battles.** Learn the patterns of tactical situations.
- **Do tactical decision games.** Practise recognising situations and choosing responses.
- **Reflect after every exercise.** What did you miss? What would you do differently?

---

## Training Your Platoon's Sensemaking

Your section commanders are sensemakers too. Train them:

- **Give them the intent.** Always explain why, not just what.
- **Use rehearsals.** Rehearsals build shared understanding. Every section commander should know what the others will do.
- **Debrief honestly.** After every exercise, discuss what was missed and why.
