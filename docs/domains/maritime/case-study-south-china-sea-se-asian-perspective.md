# South China Sea — A Southeast Asian Perspective

> *"ASEAN has never officially recognized the 2016 South China Sea Arbitral Award... yet ASEAN has repeatedly affirmed UNCLOS as the comprehensive legal framework governing all activities at sea."*
> — The Diplomat, *10 Years After the South China Sea Arbitration*

## The Dispute in Context

The South China Sea dispute involves China's expansive "nine-dash line" claim against the maritime entitlements of Brunei, Indonesia, Malaysia, the Philippines, and Vietnam. The dispute is simultaneously a legal contest, a strategic competition, and an economic contest over fisheries, hydrocarbon resources, and sea lines of communication.

From a Southeast Asian perspective, the dispute is not abstract. It shapes coastal state sovereignty, affects the livelihoods of fishing communities, determines access to energy resources, and structures the region's relationship with China—its largest economic partner and its most assertive strategic competitor.

## The 2016 Arbitral Award

On 12 July 2016, the Permanent Court of Arbitration in The Hague issued its award in the case brought by the Philippines against China under Annex VII of the United Nations Convention on the Law of the Sea (UNCLOS). The tribunal:
- Ruled that China's "nine-dash line" has no legal basis under UNCLOS.
- Clarified the maritime status of features in the Spratly Islands.
- Found that China had violated the Philippines' sovereign rights within its exclusive economic zone.

The award is final and binding. China rejected it. The United States, while not a party, supported the ruling's legal conclusions. The Philippines, under President Duterte, initially downplayed the award while pursuing closer economic ties with China. Under President Marcos Jr., Manila elevated the award into the central pillar of its South China Sea strategy.

## ASEAN's Dilemma

ASEAN's response to the award illustrates the organisation's structural constraints. At the 49th ASEAN Foreign Ministers' Meeting in Vientiane, just two weeks after the ruling, member states could not reach consensus to include any direct reference to the ruling in the joint communiqué. According to Philippine officials, every proposed reference was vetoed by a member state without explanation or counter-proposal.

This pattern persisted. Nearly a decade later, ASEAN has never issued a collective statement formally recognising or directly invoking the 2016 award. CSIS AMTI's Arbitration Support Tracker documents that, aside from the Philippines, none of the ASEAN countries have formally endorsed the tribunal's decision. Indonesia, Malaysia, Singapore, Vietnam, and Myanmar have taken a more cautious approach by only positively acknowledging the ruling.

ASEAN's consensus-based decision-making enables opponents of the award to block supportive language while supporters avoid public confrontation. This structural reality means that ASEAN's collective position remains one of studied ambiguity.

## The Philippine Perspective

For the Philippines, the award is not merely a legal victory; it is a strategic instrument. Philippine strategy has evolved through three phases:

1. **Litigation phase (2013–2016).** The Aquino administration initiated the arbitration case. The legal strategy was designed to clarify rights without immediately confronting China militarily.

2. **Accommodation phase (2016–2022).** The Duterte administration de-emphasised the award, prioritising economic relations with China. The Philippines did not abandon its legal claims, but it did not operationalise them.

3. **Implementation phase (2022–present).** The Marcos Jr. administration has sought to operationalise the award through law, practice, and diplomacy. The Philippine approach now combines:
   - **Transparency strategy.** Documenting and publicising Chinese coercive activities at sea to build international normative pressure.
   - **Legal consolidation.** Aligning domestic legislation, maritime zone definitions, and fisheries regulation with the award's findings.
   - **Diplomatic embedding.** Incorporating the award into bilateral and multilateral engagements with the United States, Japan, Australia, and ASEAN.
   - **Coalition-building.** Anchoring the award within a wider network of UNCLOS-committed states.

Philippine analysts note that the award is not self-executing. Without domestic legal consolidation and disciplined state practice at sea, its strategic potential remains underdeveloped.

## The Legal Framework: UNCLOS as Common Ground

UNCLOS provides the legal framework within which all Southeast Asian claimants operate. The ASEAN Leaders' Declaration on Maritime Cooperation, adopted in May 2026, referred to UNCLOS no fewer than 18 times—reaffirming the convention as the comprehensive legal framework governing all maritime activities.

This repeated invocation of UNCLOS is significant. It represents ASEAN's preferred mechanism for managing the dispute: not by directly confronting China's claims, but by affirming a rules-based framework that all claimants have accepted. The Code of Conduct (COC) negotiations between ASEAN and China, ongoing since 2013, are the institutional expression of this approach.

However, the COC process faces significant obstacles. China opposes linking the COC to the 2016 award. ASEAN members are divided on the legal status and binding force of a future COC. The 2026 deadline for concluding an "effective and substantive" COC is approaching with little evidence of imminent agreement.

## Grey-Zone Tactics and the Strategic Environment

China's approach to the South China Sea has evolved since 2016. The award did not alter Chinese behaviour on the ground. Instead, China expanded its use of grey-zone tactics: coast guard patrols, maritime militia presence, island reinforcement, and diplomatic coercion against Southeast Asian claimants.

CSIS AMTI documents continued Chinese activity at features such as Second Thomas Shoal, Scarborough Shoal, and Antelope Reef. Philippine vessels have been blocked, shadowed, and water-cannonned. These incidents are not merely localised harassment; they represent a sustained effort to change the facts on the water while the legal dispute remains unresolved.

The strategic environment has also shifted in ways that affect Southeast Asian calculations. Under the second Trump administration, US leadership has become less predictable. Southeast Asian states, including the Philippines, have raised concerns about US reliability. China-Southeast Asia economic integration has deepened, with China remaining the region's largest trading partner. These factors create pressure on claimant states to avoid confrontation with Beijing.

## The Southeast Asian Strategic Dilemma

Southeast Asian claimants face a fundamental dilemma. The legal framework—UNCLOS and the 2016 award—favours their claims. The strategic reality—Chinese power, economic integration, and US unpredictability—favours accommodation or silence.

This dilemma manifests differently across the region:
- **Vietnam** has adopted the most assertive legal stance within ASEAN, officially welcoming the award and using its findings to defend its own maritime rights.
- **Malaysia** and **Indonesia** have taken cautious positions, positively acknowledging the ruling without openly supporting it. Indonesia's recent recognition of an overlapping claim with China and proposal for joint development in the North Natuna Sea reflects a shift toward accommodation.
- **Brunei** remains silent, avoiding direct confrontation.
- **The Philippines** has moved from accommodation toward operationalisation, but remains constrained by material asymmetries.

## The Normative Significance of the Award

Despite limited immediate impact on Chinese behaviour, the 2016 award has reshaped the normative terrain of the South China Sea dispute. It has:
- Clarified that historic rights claims cannot override UNCLOS.
- Established that artificial islands do not generate maritime entitlements.
- Created an authoritative legal baseline against which all state actions are measured.

Every Chinese coercive act at sea is now measured against the award's findings. Beijing can dominate the water on a given day, but it struggles to dominate the normative narrative. The award has denied China the comfort of ambiguity.

## Lessons for Maritime Strategy

1. **Legal victory requires operational follow-through.** The Philippine experience demonstrates that an arbitral award is a strategic instrument, not a strategic outcome. Implementation requires legal consolidation, disciplined state practice, diplomatic embedding, and coalition-building—applied continuously over years.

2. **ASEAN consensus constrains collective action.** ASEAN's consensus mechanism enables members to avoid confrontation with China, but it also prevents the organisation from acting collectively to uphold international law. The result is a regional order managed through avoidance rather than resolution.

3. **Grey-zone tactics exploit legal ambiguity.** China's strategy depends on operating below the threshold of armed conflict while changing facts on the ground. Legal clarity—provided by UNCLOS and the award—is a necessary but insufficient counter. Physical presence and enforcement capacity must accompany legal argument.

4. **Economic dependence shapes strategic choice.** Deepening China-Southeast Asia economic ties create pressure on claimant states to avoid confrontation. This structural factor will persist regardless of legal rulings or diplomatic statements.

5. **Small and middle states need multi-layered strategy.** International law is not self-enforcing. Small states must combine legal, diplomatic, military, and economic instruments to sustain their claims. The Philippines' evolving approach—law, practice, diplomacy, alliances—represents a model of this multi-layered strategy.
