# Gulf of Guinea — Maritime Security and the Blue Economy

> *"Almost 40 per cent of the fish caught in West African waters is taken illegally."*
> — Chatham House, *Maritime Security in the Gulf of Guinea* (2013)

The Gulf of Guinea — the coastal zone stretching from Senegal to Angola — is one of the world's most significant maritime regions. It is an economic lifeline for coastal and landlocked West African states, a source of global energy production, and a hub of international trade. It is also the world's most dangerous maritime domain.

This page examines the nature of maritime insecurity in the Gulf of Guinea, its causes, consequences, and the regional architecture developed to address it.

---

## The Nature of Maritime Insecurity

### Piracy and Armed Robbery at Sea

The Gulf of Guinea is the global epicentre of maritime piracy. In 2020, more than 95 percent of global piracy incidents occurred in this region. Unlike Somali piracy, which focused on kidnap for ransom, Gulf of Guinea piracy primarily targets cargo theft. Crew kidnapping occurs but is secondary to the theft of fuel, equipment, and valuables.

Pirate attacks in the Gulf of Guinea are characterised by high levels of violence. Of the 58 incidents reported to the International Maritime Bureau in 2012, 37 involved the use of firearms. Pirates are often well-organised, well-armed, and well-funded.

### Illegal, Unreported, and Unregulated Fishing

IUU fishing is the most economically damaging maritime crime in the Gulf of Guinea. West African governments collectively lose up to USD 1.5 billion annually to IUU fishing. Foreign industrial trawlers — often Chinese, European, or Russian — operate illegally in West African waters, depleting fish stocks that sustain coastal communities.

IUU fishing is not merely an environmental issue. It is a security issue. The depletion of fish stocks undermines food security, destroys livelihoods, and drives young men toward piracy and other forms of maritime crime. The link between IUU fishing and piracy is a vicious cycle: IUU fishing destroys legitimate livelihoods, creating recruits for pirate networks.

### Oil Theft and Petro-Piracy

Nigeria loses approximately 7 percent of its oil output to theft and piracy. Criminal networks siphon crude oil from pipelines and offshore platforms, selling it on the black market. This deprives the government of revenue, causes severe environmental damage, and funds further criminal activity.

### Trafficking

The Gulf of Guinea is a major transit hub for narcotics from Latin America destined for Europe. It is also a corridor for human trafficking and weapons smuggling. Maritime crime in the Gulf of Guinea is interconnected — the same networks that traffic drugs also traffic weapons and people.

## The Yaoundé Architecture

In 2013, heads of state and government of West and Central African states met in Yaoundé, Cameroon, to address maritime insecurity. The Yaoundé Code of Conduct established a regional maritime security architecture comprising:

- **Inter-Regional Coordination Centre (CICC):** Based in Yaoundé, coordinating ECOWAS and ECCAS maritime security efforts.
- **Multinational Maritime Coordination Centres (MMCCs):** Regional centres in zones across the Gulf of Guinea.
- **National maritime security committees:** Coordinating national responses.

The Yaoundé architecture has achieved significant results. Piracy incidents declined by roughly 90 percent from their peak in 2020 to 2024. However, challenges remain: IUU fishing continues, trafficking persists, and the prosecution of suspected pirates remains extremely limited.

## The Economics of Piracy

### Direct Costs

Piracy imposes significant direct costs on maritime stakeholders. Shipping companies incur additional expenses of up to USD 50,000 per voyage due to extended routes, armed escorts, and security infrastructure. Insurance premiums increase. Port efficiency declines.

### Indirect Costs

Indirect costs include disrupted supply chains, loss of investor confidence, and reduced competitiveness of regional ports. Crude oil theft and piracy in Nigerian waters alone cost the country over USD 1.5 billion annually.

### The Criminal Economy

Piracy generates income for coastal communities where legitimate opportunities are scarce. A single successful hijacking can net hundreds of thousands of dollars — far exceeding average annual incomes in Nigeria or Cameroon. This creates a perverse incentive structure that sustains pirate networks.

## The Blue Economy Approach

The blue economy concept reframes maritime security as an economic development issue. Rather than focusing solely on naval patrols, the blue economy approach addresses the root causes of maritime crime: poverty, unemployment, and resource depletion.

Key elements include:
- Sustainable fisheries management
- Alternative livelihood programmes for former pirates
- Port infrastructure development
- Maritime domain awareness
- Regional cooperation

---

## Lessons for Maritime Operations in Africa

### 1. Maritime Security Is a Development Issue

Maritime insecurity in the Gulf of Guinea is driven by poverty, unemployment, and resource depletion. Naval patrols alone cannot solve the problem. Sustainable maritime security requires economic development.

### 2. Regional Architecture Works — But Requires Capacity

The Yaoundé architecture has reduced piracy significantly. But implementation remains uneven. Regional coordination is hampered by language barriers, equipment incompatibilities, and national rivalries.

### 3. Prosecution Is a Critical Gap

Only three piracy cases have been tried in court in the past decade — in Togo, Nigeria, and Denmark — despite 115 piracy incidents reported in 2020 alone. Without prosecution, there is no deterrence.

### 4. Land-Sea Integration Is Essential

Maritime crime in the Gulf of Guinea is organised from land. Pirate networks operate from coastal communities. Effective responses must address both the maritime and terrestrial dimensions of the problem.

### 5. International Partnerships Matter

The Gulf of Guinea is a global concern. International navies — including those of the EU, US, China, India, and Brazil — conduct joint exercises and capacity-building programmes. But these must be coordinated with regional frameworks to avoid duplication and ensure local ownership.
