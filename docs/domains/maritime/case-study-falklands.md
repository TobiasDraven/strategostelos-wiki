# Case Study — Falklands War (1982): Maritime Power in a Littoral Campaign

> *"The British victory would have to be judged anyway as a fairly close run thing... We fought our way along a knife-edge."*
> — Admiral Sir John Woodward, Task Force Commander

This case study examines the 1982 Falkland Islands campaign, the largest maritime campaign since the Second World War. It demonstrates the application of maritime power to achieve political objectives in a littoral environment.

---

## Background

On 2 April 1982, Argentine forces invaded and occupied the Falkland Islands (Islas Malvinas), a British overseas territory 275 nautical miles from Argentina and 6,700 nautical miles from Britain. The British government resolved to retake the islands by force. The campaign lasted 74 days.

---

## The Operational Problem

### British Challenges

Britain faced a severe logistical challenge. The nearest British base was Ascension Island, 3,500 nautical miles from the Falklands. The task force would have to operate 8,000 miles from home with no local infrastructure.

Britain had only two small aircraft carriers (*HMS Hermes* and *HMS Invincible*), 33 Sea Harrier V/STOL fighters, and a limited number of escorts. There was no airborne early warning (AEW) capability. The Royal Navy had been cut back in recent years and was not designed for this type of operation.

### Argentine Advantages

Argentina enjoyed significant geographic advantage. The Falklands were within range of Argentine land-based aircraft. Argentina possessed a fleet of 17 combatant ships, three submarines, and approximately 130 operational fixed-wing attack aircraft, including Dassault Super Etendards armed with Exocet anti-ship missiles.

---

## The Maritime Campaign Phase

### Deployment (April 1982)

The British task force sailed from Britain on 5 April. Rear Admiral John Woodward's carrier battle group departed Ascension Island on 18 April, arriving in the Total Exclusion Zone around the Falklands on 1 May.

### Sea Control (May 1982)

The first priority was sea control. On 2 May, the nuclear submarine *HMS Conqueror* sank the Argentine cruiser *ARA General Belgrano* with two torpedo hits. The Argentine Navy, fearful of further submarine attacks, withdrew its surface fleet to port for the remainder of the war.

This single action eliminated the Argentine surface navy from the campaign. It was a decisive application of undersea warfare.

### Air Superiority (May 1982)

The Sea Harriers flew combat air patrols over the task force and strike missions against Argentine positions. They proved highly effective, shooting down 20 Argentine aircraft without loss in air-to-air combat.

However, the British lacked AEW. Argentine aircraft could approach at low level and attack before being detected. The task force suffered losses. *HMS Sheffield* was sunk by an Exocet missile on 4 May. *HMS Coventry* was sunk by bombs on 25 May. Three other ships were damaged.

---

## The Amphibious Landing

On 21 May, the amphibious task force landed 3 Commando Brigade at San Carlos Water on East Falkland. The landing was unopposed, but Argentine aircraft attacked the ships in the anchorage.

The British established a beachhead at San Carlos and began the advance toward Port Stanley. The land campaign lasted three weeks. Port Stanley fell on 14 June, and the Argentine garrison surrendered.

---

## Lessons for the Maritime Domain

### 1. Sea Control Is Essential

British success depended on establishing sea control. Without sea control, the amphibious landing would have been impossible. The sinking of the *Belgrano* was the decisive action that secured sea control.

### 2. Submarines Are Decisive

The single submarine *HMS Conqueror* eliminated an entire navy. The undersea domain is the most asymmetric domain. A small submarine force can achieve disproportionate effects.

### 3. AEW Is Essential

The lack of AEW was the most significant British deficiency. Argentine aircraft could approach at low level and attack before being detected. AEW would have extended the detection range and increased the effectiveness of the Sea Harriers.

### 4. The Sea Harrier Was Decisive

The Sea Harrier proved that a small number of highly capable aircraft can achieve air superiority. The Sea Harrier shot down 20 Argentine aircraft without loss. It demonstrated the value of V/STOL aircraft in maritime operations.

### 5. Integrated Defence Is Required

The San Carlos anchorage was defended by an integrated system: Sea Harriers patrolled the airspace, surface ships provided missile defence, and ground forces provided close-in defence. This integrated defence defeated the Argentine air attacks.

### 6. Logistics Determine the Campaign

The campaign was a logistical feat. The task force operated 8,000 miles from home. Merchant ships were requisitioned to carry supplies and troops. The campaign demonstrated the importance of logistics in maritime operations.

### 7. The Littoral Is the Future

The Falklands campaign was a littoral campaign. The maritime, land, and air domains were closely integrated. The littoral is the most complex operational environment. Future campaigns will be fought in the littorals.
