# Battle of Midway (1942) — Intelligence and Decision

> *"I want you to be specific... After all, this is the job I have given you — to be the admiral commanding the Japanese forces, and tell me what is going on."*
> — Admiral Chester W. Nimitz to Commander Edwin T. Layton, 27 May 1942

On 4 June 1942, six months after Pearl Harbor, the United States Navy engaged the Imperial Japanese Navy at Midway Atoll. The battle is widely regarded as the turning point of the Pacific War. It was won not primarily by courage or firepower, but by intelligence, decision-making, and the effective use of limited resources.

This case study examines the battle through the first-hand accounts of Admiral Chester Nimitz (Commander-in-Chief, Pacific Fleet), Commander Edwin T. Layton (Fleet Intelligence Officer), and the official after-action reports.

---

## The Intelligence Picture

### Codebreaking

By May 1942, the U.S. Navy's codebreaking team at Pearl Harbor — led by Lieutenant Commander Joseph Rochefort and intelligence officer Edwin T. Layton — had decrypted sufficient Japanese messages to advise Nimitz of the Japanese plan to attack Midway. The critical breakthrough came from a ruse: Midley sent a plaintext message (which the Japanese intercepted) reporting problems with its fresh water plant. When Japanese intelligence referenced "AF" having water problems, the Americans confirmed that "AF" was Midway.

Layton's account, published in *And I Was There* (1985), described the moment:

> *"I want you to be specific," Nimitz said, fixing me with his cool blue eyes. "After all, this is the job I have given you — to be the admiral commanding the Japanese forces, and tell me what is going on."*

Layton told Nimitz that the carriers would probably attack on the morning of 4 June, from the northwest on a bearing of 325 degrees, sighted at about 175 miles from Midway at around 0700 hours.

### The Disagreement

There was a critical rift between Layton's team (HYPO) and Washington (OP-20-G). Washington believed that the Japanese would commit five carriers to the Midway operation, including Zuikaku. HYPO assessed four carriers, excluding Zuikaku. Nimitz's formal "Estimate of the Situation" on 26 May explicitly noted the disagreement.

Nimitz chose to trust his local intelligence team. This decision was vindicated by events: the Japanese committed four carriers (Akagi, Kaga, Hiryu, Soryu) — exactly as HYPO predicted.

---

## The Plan

### Point Luck

Nimitz positioned his three carriers (Enterprise, Hornet, and the damaged Yorkwood) at "Point Luck," 200 nautical miles northeast of Midway — outside Japanese scouting range but within striking distance. This positioning was deliberate: it gave the American carriers time and standoff room to make an informed go-no-go decision before committing to battle.

Nimitz's plan was to use Midway-based aircraft and submarines to whittle down the Japanese carrier force before committing his own carriers. His operation order stated:

> *"It is essential that enemy carrier decks be damaged to immobilize enemy planes" and, if possible, that "enemy carriers be sunk before they get within striking range of Midway."*

---

## The Battle

### The Morning of 4 June

At 0430, PBY scouts launched from Midway. At 0534, they sighted enemy carriers. At 0603, a more detailed report arrived: "2 carriers and battleships bearing 320 degrees, distance 180, course 135, speed 25 knots."

Rear Admiral Frank Jack Fletcher, commanding the carrier force, ordered Rear Admiral Raymond Spruance (TF 16) to proceed southwest and attack. Fletcher would follow as soon as his planes were recovered.

### The Afternoon

The decisive strikes came in the late afternoon. Dive bombers from Enterprise and Yorktown caught three Japanese carriers (Akagi, Kaga, and Soryu) while their flight decks were loaded with armed and fueled aircraft. All three were fatally hit. The fourth carrier, Hiryu, was sunk later that evening.

The Japanese lost all four carriers, plus 248 aircraft and 3,057 men. The Americans lost Yorktown, 145 aircraft, and 307 men.

## Lessons for Maritime Operations

### 1. Intelligence Wins Battles

The victory at Midway was made possible by codebreaking, traffic analysis, and the willingness of a commander (Nimitz) to trust his intelligence team over Washington.

### 2. Decision-Making Under Uncertainty

Nimitz made his plans with incomplete information. He did not know the exact number of Japanese carriers, the exact timing, or the exact bearing. He planned for the most likely scenario and positioned his forces to adapt as the situation developed.

### 3. Technology and Doctrine

The American victory depended on the integration of codebreaking, scouting, and strike aircraft. But technology alone was not decisive — it was the way Nimitz and his commanders used technology that mattered.

### 4. The Japanese Failure

The Japanese assumed the Americans would not be at Midway in force. Their scouting was inadequate. Their carriers were caught with loaded flight decks. The Japanese failure was a failure of imagination and assumption, not of courage.
