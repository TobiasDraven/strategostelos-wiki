# Ship Captain's Maritime Operations — Commanding the Vessel in the Maritime Domain

> *"The captain of a warship is the direct representative of the state. The captain's authority is absolute aboard the vessel."*
> — NWP 1-14M, *The Commander's Handbook on the Law of Naval Operations*

The ship captain operates at the most granular level of maritime command. The captain is responsible for the safety of the ship, the crew, and the mission. In combat, the captain must make decisions in seconds, often without consulting higher authority.

This page discusses the maritime domain at the ship level, relevant to commanding officers of warships from patrol vessels to aircraft carriers.

---

## The Captain's Maritime Environment

The captain operates in a three-dimensional battlespace. Threats can come from above (aircraft, missiles), from the surface (enemy ships), and from below (submarines, mines). The captain must maintain awareness in all three dimensions simultaneously.

### The OODA Loop at Sea

Boyd's OODA loop applies at the ship level. The captain who observes the threat first, orients correctly, decides quickly, and acts decisively gains the advantage. In a missile duel, the cycle can compress to seconds.

The captain must balance the need for emission control (avoiding detection) against the need for situational awareness (detecting the enemy). Emitting radar signals reveals the ship's position. Remaining passive risks being surprised. This is the fundamental tension of modern naval operations.

---

## The Captain's Tactical Problem

### Scouting

The captain must find the enemy before the enemy finds the ship. Scouting is the most critical process in naval combat. The captain uses organic sensors (radar, sonar, electronic warfare) and off-ship information (satellite, aircraft, other ships) to build a picture of the battlespace.

In a distributed lethality concept, each ship must be capable of independent scouting. The captain cannot rely solely on off-ship sensors. Organic sensors — shipborne radar, sonar, and electronic warfare systems — are the ship's primary means of finding the enemy.

### Weapons Employment

The captain employs weapons based on the tactical situation. Anti-ship missiles are the primary weapon for surface warfare. Surface-to-air missiles defend against air threats. Torpedoes and depth charges engage submarines. The captain must decide when to employ weapons and which weapons to use.

The decision to employ weapons is a command decision. The captain must weigh the risk of revealing the ship's position against the need to destroy the enemy. In emission control conditions, the captain may choose to remain passive until the enemy is within weapons range.

### Manoeuvre

The captain manoeuvres the ship to gain tactical advantage. Manoeuvre positions the ship for scouting and shooting. It also reduces the ship's vulnerability to enemy weapons. Evasive manoeuvring can defeat incoming missiles. High speed reduces the submarine threat.

---

## The Captain's Warfare Areas

### Surface Warfare

The captain fights surface actions using anti-ship missiles, guns, and electronic warfare. The modern surface action is a missile duel. The ship that launches first and scores hits generally wins.

### Anti-Air Warfare

The captain defends the ship against aircraft and missile attacks. Air defence is layered: outer layer (fighters), middle layer (surface-to-air missiles), inner layer (close-in weapons systems). The captain must coordinate these layers and decide when to engage.

### Anti-Submarine Warfare

The captain fights submarines using sonar, torpedoes, and depth charges. Submarines are the most difficult threat to detect and engage. The captain must maintain constant vigilance against submarine attack.

### Mine Warfare

The captain avoids mines using mine-hunting sonar and route planning. Mines are the most cost-effective sea denial weapon. A single mine can disable or sink a warship.

---

## The Captain's Legal Obligations

The captain has legal obligations under the law of the sea and the law of armed conflict. The captain must respect the sovereignty of coastal states in their territorial seas. The captain must comply with the rules of engagement. The captain must treat prisoners humanely and respect the rights of neutral vessels.

These legal obligations are not constraints on operations. They are the framework within which legitimate military operations are conducted.

---

## The Captain's Landward Problem

The captain faces a unique problem in the maritime domain. The captain must simultaneously scout for the enemy, employ weapons, manoeuvre the ship, and comply with legal obligations. These tasks are interdependent. Scouting enables effective weapons employment. Weapons employment enables manoeuvre. Manoeuvre enables continued scouting.

The captain who masters this cycle is the captain who brings the ship and crew home.
