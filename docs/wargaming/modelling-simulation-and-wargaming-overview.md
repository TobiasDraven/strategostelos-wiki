# Modelling, Simulation and Wargaming Overview

## Definitions

### Model

A model is a **scaled representation of the real world**. A topographic map is a model; an abstract sculpture is not. Models are static — they represent a situation at a specific point in time, like a photograph.

The process of distilling the real world into a model is called **modelling**. A model that serves as the underlying foundation for a simulation or wargame is also called the **game engine**.

**Fidelity** is the hallmark of a model. A map or scale model is only useful to the extent that it accurately represents reality.

### Simulation

Transitioning a model into the realm of time creates a **simulation**. An aircraft model in a wind tunnel observed over different airspeeds over time becomes a simulation.

A simulation represents a potential real-world process or system by imitating its operations. It relies on models that are iterated over time. When participants compete against each other, it becomes a **simulation game**. When armed factions are involved, it is a **wargame**.

### Wargame

A wargame is a process that imitates aspects of warfare at the tactical, operational, or strategic level. It is used to:
- Examine warfighting concepts
- Train and educate commanders and analysts
- Explore scenarios
- Assess how force planning and posture choices affect campaign outcomes

Wargames are **adversarial** and often focus on specific strategic questions.

## The Demarcation

| Feature | Simulation | Wargame |
|---------|-----------|---------|
| Adversary | No thinking human opponent | Thinking human opponent required |
| Competition | Against environment/system | Against another player |
| Example | Flight simulator, CPX | Kriegsspiel, board wargame |

> In cases where both terms are appropriate, they are used interchangeably.

## Mathematical Foundations

Mathematics equations form the foundation for a model. These models then serve as the foundation for a simulation or wargame.

### Lanchester's Laws

Lanchester's laws are differential equations describing the time-dependent strengths of two opposing armies. Developed independently by Frederick Lanchester and M. Osipov during World War I.

- **Linear Law** — for ancient hand-to-hand combat
- **Square Law** — for modern combat with long-range weapons

The square law shows that the side with more soldiers or greater firepower at the beginning will win, given equal conditions. Applied to:
- Pickett's Charge (Gettysburg, 1863)
- Battle of Britain (1940)
- Battle of Kursk
- Guerrilla warfare modelling

## Simulation Fidelity

| Fidelity | Description |
|----------|-------------|
| Low | Minimum simulation required to respond to inputs and provide outputs |
| Medium | Responds automatically to stimuli with limited accuracy |
| High | Nearly indistinguishable from the real system |
