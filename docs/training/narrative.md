# Narrative Composition

> *"Drafting the 'Narrative' for a military training exercise — often mistakenly called the 'Scenario' — is as much a science as it is an art."*
> — Adapted from EXDESIGN Textbook, Chapter 5/6

## What Is the Narrative?

The **Narrative** (also called the **General Idea**) describes the geopolitical and socio-economic backdrop of an exercise. It serves as the higher-order framework within which the exercise takes place, describing the strategic and operational-level background and telling the story up to the point where military action is required.

**Key distinction:** The Narrative sets the stage. The Scenario (Special Idea) puts the Primary Training Audience (PTA) into the tactical situation.

---

## The Seven-Part Structure

The Narrative consists of seven interconnected parts, built sequentially from foundational context to tactical mission:

```
INTRODUCTION → ORIENTATION → HISTORICAL BACKGROUND → ROAD TO WAR/CRISIS → MILITARY PROBLEM
```

### 1. Introduction

The Introduction should be kept **short**. It introduces the PTA to the narrative and touches on key aspects discussed in detail in the Historical Background and Road to War/Crisis sections.

**Best practice:** Write the Introduction **only after** the entire Narrative has been completed. This ensures it accurately reflects the content that follows.

### 2. Orientation

The PTA is introduced to the operational environment. This part is subdivided into:

#### Geographical Orientation
Describes the **physical environment** — natural features and man-made infrastructure:

| Level | Description | Map Scale |
|-------|-------------|-----------|
| **Global** | Position of the country on the world stage | 1:100,000,000 |
| **Regional** | Continental position, neighbours, oceans, mountain ranges | 1:500,000 |
| **Area of Interest** | Relevant to own force commander; outside direct influence | — |
| **Area of Influence** | Direct impact on own force operations | — |
| **Area of Operations** | Where physical operations will be executed | 1:100,000 / 1:50,000 |

#### General Orientation
Describes the **human environment** — structures and government:
- Population (size, density, distribution)
- Ethnic composition and distribution
- Religion
- Political system
- Military and security forces
- Telecommunications and media
- Economy and health infrastructure
- Geo-political relations
- Infrastructure (cities, roads, rail, airports, harbours, electricity, water, sanitation)
- Internally displaced persons (IDP) camps where applicable

> **Tip:** Use maps, charts, satellite photos, and other visual aids wherever possible.

### 3. Historical Background

The Historical Background is the **cornerstone** of the Narrative. It sets the scene for everything that follows.

**Primary aim:** Introduce the reader to human dynamics — main role players and underlying conflict triggers.

#### Main Role Players

| Type | Description |
|------|-------------|
| **Protagonists** | The "good guys" — but avoid creating an innocent party. Add negative characteristics to create moral complexity. |
| **Antagonists** | The "bad guys" or aggressors. Avoid limiting to one entity, but guard against too many parties causing confusion. |
| **Sympathisers** | Supporters of either side. Sub-divide into active supporters and moral supporters. Can include states, NGOs, political parties, ethnic groups, religious factions. |
| **Neutrals** | Parties supporting neither side. Include states, UN, Red Cross, religious groups. Useful for imposing movement restrictions. |

#### Conflict Triggers

It is best to use a **combination** of triggers rather than relying on a single cause:

| Category | Examples |
|----------|----------|
| **Historical** | Wars lost, oppression, genocide, tribal feuds |
| **Ethnic/Religious** | Race/ethnic differences, religious friction, xenophobia |
| **Geo-Political** | Border disputes, resource competition, political rivalry, election fraud, constitutional changes, ideology clashes, coup d'état |
| **Socio-Economic** | Urbanisation, overcrowding, unemployment, poverty, income inequality |

### 4. Road to War/Crisis (RTW/C)

The RTW/C is the **second major component** of the narrative. It sets the scene on the strategic/operational level by chronologically unpacking events that led to the crisis.

**Main functions:**
- Serves as an expansion of the Historical Background
- Creates context for the PTA to understand the military problem
- Acts as a **bridge** between Historical Background and Scenario

#### Road to War vs. Road to Crisis

| Type | When to Use |
|------|-------------|
| **Road to War (RTW)** | Conflict has erupted or has potential to escalate into military conflict. Describes lead-up to war, initial skirmishes, engagements, battles. |
| **Road to Crisis (RTC)** | Military conflict is not central. Used for MOOTWA, conflict resolution, electoral support, law enforcement assistance, or political crisis preceding war. Also covers natural/man-made disasters. |

#### The Escalation Curve

The RTW/C maps the **escalation of the crisis** by listing major events in chronological order that contributed to instability. Selected incidents serve as anchor points for the escalation curve.

**Strategic and Operational Escalation indicators:**
- Countries and organisations affected or involved
- Peace initiatives (national, regional, international)
- AU and UN efforts
- Declarations by regional/international bodies (UN Security Council Resolutions, embargoes, sanctions, punitive actions, travel bans, naval and air blockades, seizure of assets)

**Grassroots disruptive actions:**
- Protests, riots, public unrest
- Attacks on government infrastructure and security installations
- Rise in criminality and civil disobedience
- Declaration of martial law / curfews
- Military force concentrations, logistical stockpiling, large exercises

### 5. Military Problem

This is the **last component** of the narrative — the final step down from strategic/operational to tactical level.

#### Military Problem vs. Mission

| Term | Definition |
|------|------------|
| **Military Problem** | A broad, generalised statement indicating that military action or intervention is required to solve a defined situation. |
| **Mission** | A narrowly defined instruction or tasking to be executed within defined parameters (time and space) to achieve a specified end state. |

#### Mission Statement Structure

The Military Problem consists of **three parts**:

| Part | Description |
|------|-------------|
| **Situation** | Short, concise summary of the situation |
| **Decision** | The decision that was taken |
| **Tasking** | What must be done; can be stated as a qualified end state |

#### Structured vs. Unstructured

| Type | Description |
|------|-------------|
| **Structured** | Pre-determined mission with detailed end state given by higher HQ |
| **Unstructured** | Commander and staff must analyse the situation and formulate the mission statement |

## Drafting Approaches

### Objective-Driven Narrative
The narrative is developed **from** the stated exercise objectives. The scope, content, and focus are determined by what the exercise must achieve.

**Best for:** Training-focused exercises with clear, predefined learning outcomes.

### Situation-Driven Narrative
A fictitious, reality-based, or real-time situation is used **without** being guided by exercise objectives. The situation drives the narrative.

**Best for:** Contingency planning and operational rehearsal exercises.

> **Important:** Regardless of approach, success depends on the **skill and effort** of the narrative writer.

---

## When Is the Narrative Written?

The type, scope, objectives, available time, and development approach determine when the narrative is drafted. Four options exist:

### Option 1: Pre-EXSPEC
Narrative is drafted **prior** to the Exercise Specification Meeting.

**When to use:** Real-time situations or reality-based narratives for contingency planning.

### Option 2: Post-EXSPEC, Pre-CDC
Narrative is written **immediately after** the Exercise Specification Meeting but **before** the Concept Development Conference.

**Advantage:** The Narrative and Scenario are available to guide exercise concept development.

### Option 3: During CDC
Narrative is written **during** the Concept Development Conference to support the exercise concept.

**Challenge:** Time is limited; requires extensive consultation and liaison.

**Benefit:** Ensures the narrative aligns with the exercise concept being developed.

### Option 4: During Stage 3 (Planning)
Narrative is written **during** the detailed planning phase.

**Advantage:** Concept development is complete; MEL/MIL is fixed. The narrative is guaranteed to align with the exercise concept and cover all objectives.

**Pressure point:** The narrative writer must deliver a complete Narrative by the end of the Final Planning Conference.

> **Tip:** Option 4 is the most common approach, as it ensures alignment with a fixed MEL/MIL framework.

## The Storyboard: Planning Before Writing

Before the Narrative can be written, the layout and content of the different parts must first be planned using a **storyboard** — an adapted visual planning tool that allows creative writers to plan and structure a narrative before actually starting to write.

A storyboard ensures:
- The narrative is logically structured with events and incidents unpacked in linear, chronological order
- The writer can divide the main parts of the narrative into bite-size sections
- All required information is contained within the narrative, avoiding unnecessary repetition
- Gaps or possible omissions are identified early
- Relevant storylines requiring further development are identified early
- The step-down from strategic/operational level to tactical level is planned

**There is no prescribed format for a storyboard.** Some writers prefer graphic displays, while others prefer maps and overlays, mind maps, sketches, or written notes.

## The Seven-Step Construction Process

To simplify and standardise the process of constructing the Narrative, the following creative process has been developed:

| Step | Activity |
|------|----------|
| **Step 1** | Geographical placement — Identify the region/country/locality where the conflict or crisis will take place |
| **Step 2** | Identification and selection of conflict triggers — Create a starting point for the narrative |
| **Step 3** | Identification and development of the main role players — Define actors, their aims, capabilities, and power bases |
| **Step 4** | Conflict mapping — Identify and order incidents using timelines, mind maps, or map plotting |
| **Step 5** | Military mission — Step down from strategic/operational to tactical level |
| **Step 6** | Identify and draft support documents — UN/AU resolutions, MOUs, SOFAs, country profiles |
| **Step 7** | Draft the Narrative — Write the full narrative, keeping it concise, accurate, and clear |

## Supporting Documents

Supporting documents to the Narrative are divided into two tiers:

### Higher-Order Documents (Narrative)
- United Nations / African Union Resolutions
- Memorandum of Understanding (MOU)
- Status of Forces Agreement (SOFA)
- Campaign Directive
- Country Profiles

### Lower-Order Documents (Scenario)
- Tactical Area Studies
- Threat Assessments
- Rules of Engagement and Contact
- INTREPs and SITREPs
- Character Profiles

> **Critical rule:** No documents should be included that do not add direct value to the exercise. The temptation to include a host of strategic and operational-level supporting documents that do not directly contribute to the situational understanding of the PTA should be resisted.
