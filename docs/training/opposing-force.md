# Opposing Force — OPFOR

> *"The centre of gravity of the Scenario is the introduction and detailed unpacking of the Opposing Force (OPFOR)."*
> — Adapted from EXDESIGN Textbook, Chapter 11, Appendix A

## What Is OPFOR?

The **Opposing Force (OPFOR)** refers to all role players in the Narrative that are either actively opposing own forces from achieving their mission or contributing to the destabilisation of the current situation. OPFOR is not limited to conventional forces and may include:

- Conventional armed forces
- Non-conventional forces (insurgents, terrorists, organised crime syndicates)
- Non-human threats (natural disasters, cyber threats, chemical/biological threats)

> **Note:** The term "enemy" becomes ambiguous during Peace Support Operations. Terms such as "belligerents," "spoilers," "undocumented armed groups," or "rebels" may be more appropriate. For training purposes, the term OPFOR is generally preferred.

---

## Colour Coding of OPFOR

To assist the scenario writer in developing opposing forces and managing individual story lines, the OPFOR can be colour-coded:

| Colour | Description | Examples |
|--------|-------------|----------|
| **Red** | Formally declared enemy. Actively and aggressively opposes own forces. | Conventional armed forces of countries, recognised armed groups declared hostile |
| **Orange** | Does not actively oppose own forces but has a negative influence on stability and security. | "Belligerents" or "spoilers" |
| **Black/Purple** | Criminal groups. | Organised crime syndicates, smuggling rings (weapons, drugs, humans), street gangs |
| **Yellow** | Forces or role players that have not yet been classified. Identity, aim, intention, and alliances still unknown. | Unidentified armed groups |

## Constructing a Conventional Opposing Force

The following key aspects should be considered when designing a conventional opposing force:

### Posture

A clear distinction must be made whether the OPFOR is in a **defensive** or **offensive** posture, as this impacts all subsequent development.

### Specification of Size

The size of the opposing force in direct contact or opposition to the PTA must be specified:

| Category | Examples |
|----------|----------|
| **Single entity** | Sniper, vessel, aircraft |
| **Defined Military Grouping** | Section, platoon, company, battalion, regiment, brigade, task force |

### Identification

OPFOR elements may be identified by unit name and designation, or this detail may be omitted to create a training opportunity (provided later via inject or INTREP).

### Level of Command

Three levels of command can be identified:

| Level | Description |
|-------|-------------|
| **On the Front** | Force in direct contact with own forces. Most detail required. |
| **One Level Up** | Next higher level of command. Less technical detail; higher tactical context. |
| **Two Levels Up** | Operational or military strategic level. |

### Aim and Intention

- **Aim:** What objective is to be achieved (the "What")
- **Intention:** The method or approach used to achieve the aim (the "How")

There must be synergy between levels — subordinate commander's aim and intention must fit in with and support the next higher commander's.

### Composition

Composition refers to the make-up of the force in terms of capabilities (e.g., "a brigade-size force consisting of infantry, artillery, armoured cars, anti-tank, reconnaissance, air defence, and engineer capabilities").

### Force Structure

Force structure refers to the broad grouping and organisation of capabilities into units. A **bottom-up approach** is recommended — begin with the force on the front and work up to the next level(s).

### Weapons and Equipment List

A list of all major weapons systems, vehicles, aircraft, vessels, and support equipment must be linked to the units depicted on the OPFOR force structure. This information is captured in the **OPFOR Weapon and Equipment Booklet** (part of the Red Book).

Additional detail may include:
- **Serviceability levels** — percentage serviceable and forecast of additional weapons becoming available
- **Procurement** — certain weapon systems purchased on the international market and becoming operational within a specific period

### Order of Battle (ORBAT)

The ORBAT refers to the manner in which the OPFOR units have been grouped to execute a specific task. Groupings must be both realistic (logic) and balanced.

### Deployment/Disposition

Deployment refers to the tactical placement of the OPFOR in the area of operations. A detailed analysis of both macro and micro terrain must be conducted to ensure deployments are tactically sound.

### Doctrine and Tactics

- For **real-world and reality-based scenarios:** Research and obtain copies of real doctrine of the envisaged OPFOR. Refrain from generating fictitious doctrine.
- For **fictional scenarios:** More leeway is available, but care must be taken to maintain realism.

> **Critical rule:** The temptation to state that OPFOR will use own doctrine and tactics must be resisted at all costs. It is unrealistic and stifles creativity.

### Skill

Soldier skill levels can be classified into broad categories:

| Category | Description |
|----------|-------------|
| **Conscript** | Little or no training; ignorant of basic military skills |
| **Trained** | Received military training but lack combat experience |
| **Veteran/Experienced** | Battle-hardened, well trained, steadfast under fire |
| **Fanatical** | Willing to fight to the last bullet |

### Morale/Motivation

| Category | Description |
|----------|-------------|
| **Reluctant/Demotivated** | Lack motivation; likely to break under pressure |
| **Confident** | Confident in themselves, equipment, and leaders |
| **Highly Motivated (Fearless)** | Very high morale; will fight to the last man |

## Constructing a Non-Conventional Opposing Force

Non-conventional forces or destabilisers are generally structured less rigidly than conventional forces. Key aspects include:

| Aspect | Description |
|--------|-------------|
| **Name and Abbreviation** | Official name and abbreviation of the group |
| **Nickname** | Alternative name by which the group is known |
| **Recognition Markings** | Visual identifiers used by members |
| **Leader** | Name of primary leader or command structure |
| **Approximate Strength** | Estimated number of fighters/members |
| **Objectives** | Reason for existence; aligned with narrative and scenario |
| **Organisation** | Broad organisation or structure (loosely organised to well-disciplined decentralised cells) |
| **Area of Operations** | Specified area as indicated in the narrative/scenario |
| **Command Structure** | Centralised (single leader/council) or decentralised (small groups under local warlords) |
| **Weapons** | Range of weapons and ordnance at the group's disposal |
| **Modus Operandi** | Broad tactics and techniques used during past actions |
| **Support** | Popular support, regional/international support, financing, military equipment |
| **Affiliations/Associations/Alliances** | Groups sympathetic to the cause or with similar aims |

## Non-State Armed Groups/Militia

Non-state armed groups are volunteer structures formed by local communities to ensure their safety against violent attacks. Members are civilian volunteers acting as self-protection units (SPU) or self-defence units (SDU).

These groups are common in regions where extremist groups are active. Examples include the "Mayi-Mayi" in the eastern DRC, the "Janjaweed" in Sudan, and the "Anti-Balaka" in the Central African Republic.

Typical activities include:
- Acting as local neighbourhood watch or community guards
- Patrolling and setting up roadblocks and checkpoints
- Searching vehicles and people at town entrances
- Providing escorts while community members collect wood or work in the fields
- Acting as guides, interpreters, and informers to formal security structures
