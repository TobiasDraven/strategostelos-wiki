# Scenario Development — The Special Idea

> *"The Scenario or 'Special Idea' provides the tactical backdrop for the exercise — figuratively speaking, the scenario can be seen as the 'sandbox' within which the exercise will take place."*
> — Adapted from EXDESIGN Textbook, Chapter 11

## Definition

The **Scenario** (also called the **Special Idea**) provides the tactical backdrop for the exercise. It serves as the point of entry to the tactical level and provides the Primary Training Audience (PTA) with the necessary details to orient themselves with the situation and plan and execute specific actions within the set exercise framework.

> *"Detailed information on circumstances and events that led to a specific hypothetical crisis situation."*

---

## Types of Scenarios

| Type | Description |
|------|-------------|
| **Simplistic Scenarios** | Used to set the scene for a simple problem. Limited background detail; focuses on a specific topic, incident, or action. |
| **Complex Scenarios** | Used for advanced training requiring simultaneous actions on more than one level or area. Elaborate, with high volumes of facts and technical data. Often serves as the foundation for several independent or consecutive problems. |

---

## Scenario Classification

| Classification | Description |
|----------------|-------------|
| **Real-world Scenarios** | Based on actual operations or conflict situations. May be politically and operationally sensitive. |
| **Reality-based Scenarios** | Based on real-world geography, infrastructure, and role players but with fictional or hypothetical events. Simulates possible situations a force may face. |
| **Fictional Scenarios** | Based entirely on fictional situations, role players, and countries. Can be modelled to suit specific training objectives. Care must be taken to maintain realism. |

---

## Setting

The depth and realism of the training scenario is influenced by the aim and objective of the exercise. Scenarios can be drafted from three distinctive settings in time:

| Setting | Focus | Example |
|---------|-------|---------|
| **Pre-conflict/Crisis** | Build-up to conflict or crisis. Intelligence gathering, operational planning, deployment, logistical support. | Tensions rising between neighbouring countries over a disputed border; PTA tasked to plan deployment. |
| **Mid-conflict/Crisis** | Active phase of conflict. Real-time decision making, military problem solving, tactical manoeuvre, execution of operations. | Enemy formations have crossed the border and are advancing; PTA must delay the advance. |
| **Post-conflict/Crisis** | Main crisis has passed. Peacekeeping, reform, disaster management. | Hurricane has devastated a town; PTA must manage humanitarian crisis. |

## Development Framework

A well-structured scenario follows a developmental framework to ensure logical sequencing, sufficient detail, and grouped information. The framework includes:

### Part 1: Introduction

Serves as the stepping-down mechanism from the operational level (Narrative) to the tactical level. Indicates:
- **Time now** — current time and date
- **Current location of own forces** — deployment areas to orient the PTA

### Part 2: Current Situation

Provides detailed information on the current tactical situation, focusing on current events, incidents, flare-ups, hot spots, and attacks. Must be annotated on maps covering the Joint Area of Operations (JAO) and the Area of Operations (AO).

### Part 3: Opposing Force (OPFOR)

The centre of gravity of the scenario. Includes detailed descriptions of:
- Conventional forces
- Non-conventional forces (insurgents, terrorists, organised crime)
- Destabilisers
- Personality Profiles of key OPFOR commanders

### Part 4: Own Forces

Real-time force structure and organisation of own forces (blue forces). May be included in the scenario or attached as a supplementary annex (the "Blue Book"). Also includes allied/host country forces (neutral/green forces).

### Part 5: Tactical Mission

The task the PTA is to execute. The mission must clearly indicate:
- **Who** must do **what**, **where**, **by when**, **in order to achieve what**, and **do what thereafter**

### Part 6: Supporting Documents

Supplementary materials to add value to the scenario:
- Intelligence Reports (INTREPs)
- Intelligence Summaries (INTSUMs)
- Media articles
- Other open source material

---

## Reference Material — Colour-Coded Annexes

For larger, more complex exercises, several independent annexes may be created:

| Annex | Colour | Content |
|-------|--------|---------|
| **Red Book** | Red | OPFOR detail — too much to include in the main scenario |
| **Blue Book** | Blue | Own forces and allied/host country forces |
| **Green Book** | Green | Host nation and allied forces within the Joint Operational Area |
| **White Book** | White | Neutrals — local population, media, NGOs, local and regional role players |

---

## Script Writing

Script writing is a specialised area of scenario writing used to define a specific role and behaviour required of a character when personal interaction forms part of an event through role-playing.

Scripts guide role players in terms of questions to be asked or answers to be provided. Examples include role-playing as:
- A rebel leader with whom negotiations must be conducted
- A hostage-taker during hostage negotiations
- A visiting dignitary
- A news correspondent asking questions during a media briefing
- A captured OPFOR commander to be questioned
