# The Security-Development Nexus in Sub-Saharan Africa

> *"The scourge of conflicts in Africa constitutes a major impediment to the socio-economic development of the continent."*
> — Constitutive Act of the African Union

The relationship between security and development in Sub-Saharan Africa is not linear — it is a complex, dynamic system in which each dimension shapes and is shaped by the other. This page examines the security-development nexus through an African lens, drawing on the African Union's frameworks and academic research.

---

## The Conceptual Framework

### The Traditional View

The traditional view treats security and development as separate domains. Security is about military force and state stability. Development is about economic growth and poverty reduction. In this view, security is a precondition for development — you cannot develop without peace.

### The African Union's View

The African Union's Constitutive Act recognises the interdependence of peace and security with development. Article 3 states that the AU shall "promote peace, security, and stability on the continent" and "promote sustainable development at the economic, social and civic levels." These are not separate objectives — they are mutually reinforcing.

### The Human Security Approach

The UNDP's concept of human security broadens the definition of security beyond state security to include:
- **Freedom from want:** Economic security, food security, health security
- **Freedom from fear:** Personal security, community security, political security
- **Freedom to live in dignity:** Human rights, rule of law, participation

In the African context, human security is particularly relevant. Most Africans face threats not from foreign invasion but from poverty, disease, environmental degradation, and internal conflict. The African Union's African Human Security Index integrates these dimensions.

## The Empirical Evidence

### The Causality Debate

Does development drive security, or does security drive development? Recent research using panel data from 33 African countries (2004–2019) reveals a nuanced picture:

- **In lower-income and lower-middle-income countries:** Economic development drives national security. Growth reduces conflict vulnerability.
- **In upper-middle-income countries:** National security drives economic development. Stability enables investment and growth.

This finding has important policy implications. In the poorest countries, development interventions are security interventions. In more developed countries, security enables further development.

### The Cost of Conflict

The World Bank estimates that a civil war costs the average developing country roughly 30 years of GDP growth. Countries in protracted crises fall over 20 percentage points behind in poverty reduction. Between 1990 and 2005, conflict-related GDP losses across Africa totalled approximately USD 284 billion.

### The Peace Dividend

Conversely, peace enables development. Stabilised post-conflict zones attract increased investment and aid. Governments can shift spending from military operations to health, education, and infrastructure. The African Development Bank estimates that African countries lose up to 2 percent of GDP annually to conflict.

## The AU Policy Framework

### Post-Conflict Reconstruction and Development (PCRD)

The AU's PCRD policy, first adopted in 2006 and comprehensively revised in 2024, provides a practical framework for operationalising the security-development nexus. The revised policy is structured around eight interlinked pillars:

1. **Security:** DDRRR, security sector reform, small arms control
2. **Humanitarian Assistance:** Emergency relief, protection of civilians
3. **Governance:** Constitutional reform, elections, rule of law
4. **Socio-Economic Recovery:** Infrastructure, livelihoods, economic recovery
5. **Human Rights and Justice:** Transitional justice, reconciliation
6. **Gender:** Women's participation, gender-based violence prevention
7. **Youth:** Youth employment, education, participation
8. **Environmental Security:** Climate adaptation, natural resource management

### The African Peace and Security Architecture (APSA)

APSA integrates conflict prevention, peacebuilding, and development. The Continental Early Warning System (CEWS) monitors both conflict and development indicators. The Panel of the Wise engages in preventive diplomacy. The African Standby Force provides rapid response capability.

### The African Governance Architecture (AGA)

AGA complements APSA by addressing governance — the institutional foundation for both security and development. The African Peer Review Mechanism (APRM) assesses governance performance. The African Charter on Democracy, Elections, and Governance sets standards.

## Challenges

### The Implementation Gap

Despite comprehensive policy frameworks, implementation remains weak. The gap between policy and practice is driven by:
- **Institutional fragmentation:** Peace and security policymakers and development policymakers operate in separate silos
- **Divergent working methods:** Military and development actors have different cultures, timelines, and metrics
- **Donor dependency:** African institutions rely on external funding, which shapes priorities
- **Limited capacity:** Most African states lack the institutional capacity to implement complex, multi-dimensional programmes

### The Climate-Security Link

Climate change is a threat multiplier in Africa. The Lake Chad Basin illustrates the interaction: environmental degradation, resource scarcity, and livelihood collapse create conditions for state fragility, human insecurity, and violent extremism. The revised PCRD policy's environmental sustainability pillar addresses this link.

### The Digital Dimension

Digital technologies create both opportunities and risks. Mobile money drives financial inclusion but creates cybersecurity risks. Social media enables political participation but facilitates disinformation. The security-development nexus must account for the digital dimension.

## Lessons for Military Practitioners

### 1. Security and Development Are Inseparable

Military operations in Africa cannot be separated from development. Peacekeeping missions that ignore economic recovery and governance will not achieve sustainable peace.

### 2. Local Ownership Is Essential

Externally imposed solutions fail. African-led approaches, grounded in African knowledge systems and institutional realities, are more effective and more sustainable.

### 3. Prevention Is Cheaper Than Response

Conflict prevention costs a fraction of conflict response. Early warning, preventive diplomacy, and development interventions reduce the need for military operations.

### 4. The Anthropocene Changes Everything

Climate change, resource depletion, and environmental degradation are reshaping the security landscape. Military practitioners must understand these dynamics and integrate them into planning.

### 5. Youth Are the Key

Africa's youth population is both an opportunity and a risk. Youth unemployment drives conflict. Youth engagement in peacebuilding and development creates resilience. The security-development nexus must centre youth.
