    Electronic Warfare — General Overview¶

    "Electronic warfare is one of the five core capabilities of information operations. EW is waged to secure and maintain the freedom of action in the electromagnetic spectrum." — JP 3-13.1, Electronic Warfare

    Definition and Scope¶

    Electronic warfare (EW) is military action using electromagnetic energy to control the electromagnetic spectrum or attack the enemy. It is both offensive and defensive. Its purpose is to secure and maintain freedom of action in the electromagnetic spectrum for friendly forces while denying the same to the adversary.

    EW is not a niche capability. It is integral to modern military operations. Communications, navigation, radar, precision-guided munitions, and command and control all depend on the electromagnetic spectrum. Whoever controls the spectrum controls the battlefield.

    Historical Evolution¶

    Early Origins¶

    Electronic warfare began with the advent of radio communications in the early twentieth century. During the First World War, both sides monitored enemy radio traffic and attempted to jam communications. The discipline remained relatively simple — limited to communications jamming and direction finding.

    The Second World War¶

    The Second World War transformed EW. Radar, radio navigation, and electronic fuzes created new vulnerabilities and new targets. The Battle of the Atlantic saw extensive use of radio direction finding and radar countermeasures. The British developed "window" (chaff) to confuse German radar. Electronic deception became a sophisticated art.

    The Cold War¶

    The Cold War drove rapid advancement. The Soviet Union invested heavily in integrated air defence systems, including surface-to-air missiles and early warning radar networks. NATO developed suppression of enemy air defences (SEAD) doctrine to counter these threats. The Arab-Israeli conflicts of 1967 and 1973 demonstrated the decisive impact of electronic warfare.

    The Modern Era¶

    The 1982 Bekaa Valley operation proved that electronic warfare could enable a small air force to destroy a large integrated air defence network. The 1991 Gulf War demonstrated the integration of EW with precision strike. The ongoing Russia-Ukraine conflict has shown both the power and the limitations of modern EW.

    The Three Divisions of Electronic Warfare¶

    Electronic Support (ES)¶

    Electronic support is the passive detection, identification, and location of enemy electromagnetic emissions. It provides the foundation for situational awareness in the electromagnetic environment.

    Capabilities: - Search, intercept, identify, and locate enemy radars, communications, and other emitters. - Provide immediate threat warning (e.g., radar warning receivers alert aircrews to missile threats). - Support targeting by providing emitter locations for attack. - Feed intelligence databases with enemy radar and communication signatures.

    Key systems: - Radar warning receivers (RWR) - Electronic support measures (ESM) - Signals intelligence (SIGINT) platforms - Passive RF sensors

    Electronic Attack (EA)¶

    Electronic attack uses electromagnetic energy to degrade, neutralize, or destroy enemy combat capability. It includes jamming, deception, and anti-radiation weapons.

    Capabilities: - Jamming: Transmit noise or deceptive signals to prevent enemy receivers from detecting intended signals. Can target communications, radar, GPS, or other systems. - Deception: Inject false information into enemy systems (e.g., false radar targets, false GPS positions). - Anti-radiation weapons: Missiles that home on enemy radar emissions, destroying the radar itself. - Directed energy: High-power microwaves or lasers that damage enemy electronics.

    Key systems: - Communications jammers - Radar jammers - GPS jammers/spoofers - Anti-radiation missiles (AGM-88 HARM) - High-power microwave weapons

    Electronic Protection (EP)¶

    Electronic protection protects friendly personnel, facilities, and equipment from enemy electromagnetic effects — both enemy EW and friendly electromagnetic interference.

    Capabilities: - Technical measures: Frequency hopping, spread spectrum, directional antennas, power management. - Operational measures: Emission control (EMCON), frequency management, spectrum deconfliction. - Hardening: Shielding and filtering to protect electronics from high-power microwave or electromagnetic pulse effects.

    Key Technologies¶

    Anti-Radiation Missiles¶

    Anti-radiation missiles home on enemy radar emissions. They provide a direct kill capability against enemy air defence radars. The AGM-88 HARM is the standard NATO anti-radiation missile. Modern variants can remember target locations even if the radar shuts down.

    Unmanned Aerial Systems¶

    Unmanned aerial systems (UAS) have transformed EW. Small, cheap drones can: - Act as decoys to stimulate enemy radars. - Carry EW payloads (jammers, sensors). - Provide persistent surveillance of the electromagnetic environment. - Deliver precision strikes against EW targets.

    Software-Defined Systems¶

    Modern EW systems increasingly use software-defined architectures. This allows rapid updates to threat libraries and response techniques without hardware modifications. Software-defined EW can adapt to emerging threats faster than legacy hardware-dependent systems.

    Artificial Intelligence and Machine Learning¶

    AI is revolutionizing electronic warfare. AI systems can: - Analyze signal characteristics and identify anomalies. - Classify threats faster than human operators. - Adapt jamming responses in real time. - Prioritize targets in complex electromagnetic environments. - Operate autonomously in denied or degraded communications environments.

    Case Studies¶

    Case Study 1: Operation Focus — The Six-Day War (1967)¶

    Background: In June 1967, Israel faced a coordinated multi-front threat from Egypt, Jordan, and Syria. The Israeli Air Force (IAF) was outnumbered by the combined Arab air forces.

    The operation: At 07:45 on June 5, the IAF launched Operation Focus — a preemptive strike against Egyptian airfields. The operation exploited carefully timed surprise. Israeli aircraft flew predictable morning patrols for two years, training Egyptian radar operators to see that signature as routine. On June 5, light trainers replicated that profile while the real strike force launched and flew at low altitude to avoid radar detection.

    Results: The first wave destroyed 186 Egyptian aircraft in 80 minutes. By noon, the Egyptian Air Force had effectively ceased to exist. Jordanian and Syrian air forces suffered the same fate when they entered the conflict. By day's end, Israel had destroyed over 293 Egyptian aircraft, 50 Syrian, 20 Jordanian, and 10 Iraqi — losing fewer than 20 aircraft in return.

    EW lessons: The operation demonstrated the decisive impact of operational deception, intelligence exploitation, and compression of tactical timelines. It also created a dangerous complacency. Israel's success bred institutional overconfidence that left it unprepared for the 1973 Yom Kippur War.

    Case Study 2: The Yom Kippur War and the Bekaa Valley (1973–1982)¶

    Background: The 1973 Yom Kippur War exposed the vulnerability of Israeli air power to dense, integrated Arab surface-to-air missile networks. Egyptian and Syrian SAM belts — built around SA-2, SA-3, and SA-6 systems — inflicted severe attrition on the IAF in the opening days. Israel lost dozens of aircraft in the first three days alone.

    The response: Israel spent a decade developing electronic warfare capabilities to counter the SAM threat. The result was Operation Mole Cricket 19 in June 1982.

    The operation: On June 9, 1982, the IAF attacked 19 Syrian SA-6 batteries in Lebanon's Bekaa Valley. The kill chain relied on unmanned decoy drones (Scout and Mastiff) that stimulated Syrian radars into activating. E-2C Hawkeye aircraft relayed targeting data to F-4 Phantoms firing anti-radiation missiles. Within two hours, 17 of 19 SAM batteries were destroyed. Syrian fighter scrambles were met by Israeli F-15s and F-16s with E-2C cueing. The resulting air battle destroyed 82–90 Syrian aircraft with zero Israeli losses.

    EW lessons: The Bekaa Valley operation proved that a Western-equipped air force could destroy a Soviet-built SAM belt through integrated electronic warfare, deception, and real-time data links. It established the template for modern SEAD doctrine. The AGM-88 HARM and modern loitering munitions trace their lineage directly to the tactics proven in 1982.

    Case Study 3: The Battle of Latakia (1973)¶

    Background: During the Yom Kippur War, the Israeli Navy faced Syrian and Egyptian missile boats armed with Soviet SS-N-2 Styx anti-ship missiles. The Styx had already sunk an Israeli destroyer in 1967.

    The engagement: Israeli Sa'ar-class missile boats engaged Syrian missile boats off the coast of Latakia on October 7, 1973. The Israeli boats were equipped with indigenously developed electronic warfare systems — jamming and chaff rockets designed to confuse the Styx missile's radar seeker.

    Results: More than 50 Styx missiles were fired at Israeli boats. None hit. All were deflected by chaff and jamming. Israeli Gabriel missiles sank 44 Egyptian and Syrian ships without any Israeli losses.

    EW lessons: The Battle of Latakia was the first naval engagement conducted entirely with missile combat and electronic warfare deception. It proved that electronic countermeasures could defeat anti-ship missiles — a lesson that remains relevant today. The Israeli innovations in chaff and radar jamming set a precedent still employed by navies worldwide.

    Case Study 4: Russia-Ukraine Conflict (2022–Present)¶

    Background: Russia invaded Ukraine in February 2022, deploying sophisticated EW systems including Krasukha-4 (radar jamming), Leer-3 (cellular network disruption), and Murmansk-BN (strategic electronic disruption).

    Russian EW performance: In the initial phase, Russian EW was less effective than expected. Lack of air superiority, slow advance, dense electromagnetic environment, and Ukrainian use of NATO-supplied SINCGARS radios (frequency-hopping, jam-resistant) limited Russian EW effectiveness. However, as the conflict became more static, Russian EW efforts consolidated. By 2023, Russian jamming was causing GPS-guided weapons (Excalibur shells, JDAMs, GMLRS missiles) to miss targets.

    Ukrainian countermeasures: Ukraine responded with innovation and agility. Frequency-hopping radios, encrypted communications, and Starlink satellite terminals provided resilient command and control. Ukraine developed low-cost, rapidly deployable EW solutions including handheld jammers. Modified commercial drones resisted jamming. Indigenous EW systems like Bukovel-AD countered Russian drones.

    EW lessons: The conflict demonstrates that sophisticated EW systems can disrupt operations but do not guarantee victory. Ukrainian innovation and adaptation neutralized many Russian EW advantages. The conflict also highlights the vulnerability of GPS-dependent weapons to jamming and the importance of resilient communications infrastructure. The integration of commercial technologies and asymmetric responses can level the playing field against a numerically superior EW force.

    Future Trends¶

    AI and Autonomy¶

    Artificial intelligence is enabling autonomous EW systems that can detect, analyze, and respond to threats without human intervention. In 2026, L3Harris and Shield AI completed the first flight test of an autonomous EW system that detected and maneuvered around electromagnetic threats without human input. AI is compressing the sensor-to-decision cycle from minutes to seconds.

    Directed Energy¶

    High-energy lasers and high-power microwave weapons are approaching operational maturity. Lockheed Martin predicts laser air defences will be operational before 2030. High-power microwave systems (e.g., the US Marine Corps HAVOC programme) can disable drone swarms in a single pulse — fundamentally altering the economics of counter-drone warfare.

    Software-Defined Architectures¶

    The shift from hardware-dependent to software-defined EW systems allows rapid capability updates. Modern EW platforms can be reprogrammed to counter emerging threats without hardware modifications — a critical advantage in fast-evolving electromagnetic environments.

    Integrated Multi-Domain Operations¶

    EW is increasingly integrated with cyber operations, space operations, and kinetic strike under the CEMA framework. The future battlefield will see seamless coordination of electronic attack, cyber effects, and precision strike — all synchronized through AI-enabled battle management systems.

    Implications for Operations¶

    • EW is not optional. Every modern operation requires electronic warfare planning and integration.
    • The electromagnetic spectrum must be treated as a battlespace. Commanders must plan for control of the spectrum as they plan for terrain.
    • Resilience is essential. Forces must be trained and equipped to operate when the spectrum is denied or degraded.
    • Innovation outpaces procurement. Adversaries continuously evolve their EW capabilities. Forces must be equally agile in developing countermeasures.
    • Commercial technologies matter. Smartphones, commercial drones, and civilian communications systems create both vulnerabilities and opportunities in the electromagnetic domain.
