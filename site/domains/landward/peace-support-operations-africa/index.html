    Peace Support Operations in Sub-Saharan Africa — The Austere Environment¶

    "Virtually anywhere the brigade operates will be an austere environment, with little or no viable infrastructure and food supply." — LSE, Building an Effective African Standby Force

    Sub-Saharan Africa is the primary theatre for peace support operations (PSOs) worldwide. The United Nations deploys seven of its twelve peace operations in Africa. The African Union and regional economic communities (RECs) have conducted over a dozen peace operations since 2003. These operations share a common characteristic: they are conducted in austere environments with minimal infrastructure, limited logistics, and complex political dynamics.

    This page examines the nature of peace support operations in Sub-Saharan Africa, their challenges, and the lessons they offer for military practitioners.

    The Nature of the Operating Environment¶

    The Tyranny of Distance¶

    Sub-Saharan Africa is vast. The Democratic Republic of the Congo alone is the size of Western Europe, with fewer than 500 kilometres of paved roads. Deploying forces across such distances requires strategic airlift that most African militaries do not possess. The UN depends on donor nations for airlift support, but this cannot always be guaranteed.

    The Austere Environment¶

    Most PSOs in Africa operate in environments with: - Minimal infrastructure: Few paved roads, limited airfields, unreliable power grids - Limited food and water supply: Forces must be self-sustaining - High disease burden: Malaria, Ebola, cholera, and other endemic diseases - Extreme weather: Monsoon rains, desert heat, tropical humidity - Complex political dynamics: Multiple armed groups, weak state institutions, competing national interests

    The Human Security Challenge¶

    Civilians are the primary victims of conflict in Africa. PSOs are mandated to protect civilians, but this is a complex task. Armed groups hide among the population. State security forces are often the perpetrators of abuse. Humanitarian access is restricted by insecurity, geography, and bureaucratic obstacles.

    The African Peace and Security Architecture¶

    The African Union¶

    The African Union (AU) established the African Peace and Security Architecture (APSA) in 2002. APSA comprises: - The Peace and Security Council (PSC): The decision-making body for conflict prevention, management, and resolution - The African Standby Force (ASF): A multinational standby force for rapid deployment - The Continental Early Warning System (CEWS): For conflict prevention - The Panel of the Wise: For preventive diplomacy

    Regional Economic Communities¶

    The AU's security architecture is built on regional pillars: - ECOWAS: Economic Community of West African States - ECCAS: Economic Community of Central African States - SADC: Southern African Development Community - IGAD: Intergovernmental Authority on Development - EAC: East African Community

    Each REC has its own security mechanisms and standby forces. The relationship between the AU and RECs is governed by the principle of subsidiarity — regional organisations take the lead, with the AU providing coordination and support.

    The Logistics Challenge¶

    The Dependency Trap¶

    Most African troop-contributing countries (TCCs) cannot deploy and sustain their contingents independently. They depend on external partners for: - Strategic airlift: Transporting forces to the theatre - Equipment: Vehicles, communications, medical facilities - Sustainment: Food, water, fuel, ammunition - Training: Pre-deployment training and mission-specific skills

    This dependency creates vulnerabilities. When external partners withdraw support, missions struggle to sustain themselves.

    The Mobile Rear¶

    The UN has developed the concept of a "mobile rear" — a logistics structure that enables sustained operations in austere environments. The UN Regional Service Centre in Entebbe, Uganda, serves as a hub for administrative, logistical, and technological support to UN missions in Africa.

    Lessons from MONUSCO¶

    The UN Organization Stabilization Mission in the Democratic Republic of the Congo (MONUSCO) is the largest and most expensive UN peacekeeping mission. With over 16,000 personnel and an annual budget of USD 1.1 billion, it offers critical lessons:

    1. Robust Mandates Work — But Have Costs¶

    MONUSCO's Force Intervention Brigade (FIB) was the first UN peacekeeping unit mandated to conduct offensive operations against armed groups. The FIB has been more effective at protecting civilians than traditional peacekeepers. But robust mandates compromise neutrality and can make the mission a party to the conflict.

    2. Technology Is Essential¶

    MONUSCO pioneered the use of unmanned aerial vehicles (UAVs) in UN peacekeeping. UAVs provide real-time intelligence, monitor armed group movements, and enhance situational awareness. But technology alone is insufficient — it must be integrated with ground operations and community engagement.

    3. Community Trust Is Operational Infrastructure¶

    In eastern DRC, community trust is not a "soft" variable — it is operational infrastructure. Peacekeepers need trust to move, gather early warning, and support civilian protection. Humanitarian actors need trust to deliver aid. Health workers need trust to conduct surveillance and vaccination.

    4. Withdrawal Requires Planning¶

    MONUSCO's drawdown has raised concerns about the mission's capacity to protect civilians. Withdrawal must be phased, responsible, and planned. The transfer of responsibilities to national authorities requires time and capacity-building.

    The Security-Development Nexus¶

    Peace support operations in Africa cannot be separated from development. The World Bank estimates that a civil war costs the average developing country roughly 30 years of GDP growth. Countries in protracted crises fall over 20 percentage points behind in poverty reduction.

    The AU's Post-Conflict Reconstruction and Development (PCRD) policy recognises this link. The revised 2024 policy framework integrates eight pillars: security, humanitarian assistance, governance, socio-economic recovery, human rights and justice, gender, youth, and environmental security.
